A blueprint for a garage fits on one sheet of paper, yet a framer can build every wall from it. The secret is the **scale**: each length on the drawing always stands for the same real length. In this lesson you will use scales to go between drawings and real life, and see what happens to area and volume when everything is enlarged.

## Reading a scale

:::definition Scale
A **scale** compares a length on a drawing or model with the matching real length. You will see it written three ways:
- in words: $1\ \text{cm}$ represents $2\ \text{m}$
- as a ratio, drawing : real, with **both parts in the same unit**: $1 : 50$
- with two units, as on an architect's drawing: $\tfrac14\ \text{in} : 1\ \text{ft}$
:::

To find a real length, multiply the drawing length by how much one drawing unit stands for. Then change the units if you need to.

:::example A floor plan
The floor plan above has a scale of $1 : 50$. The living-room wall is $14.1\ \text{cm}$ long on the plan. How long is the real wall, in metres?
@step $1 : 50$ means $1\ \text{cm}$ on the plan is $50\ \text{cm}$ in real life.
@step $14.1 \times 50 = 705\ \text{cm}$
@step There are $100\ \text{cm}$ in a metre: $705 \div 100 = 7.05$
@answer The wall is $7.05\ \text{m}$ long.
:::

:::mistake Mixing units in a ratio
A ratio scale uses the same unit on both sides. $1 : 50$ does **not** mean $1\ \text{cm}$ to $50\ \text{m}$. Multiply first, then convert: $705\ \text{cm} = 7.05\ \text{m}$.
:::

## Drawing lengths and imperial scales

To go from real life to the drawing, **divide** by what one drawing unit stands for.

:::example Drawing a garage wall
Noor is drawing a garage plan where $1\ \text{cm}$ represents $0.5\ \text{m}$. The back wall is $7.5\ \text{m}$ long. How long should Noor draw it?
@step Each centimetre on the drawing stands for $0.5\ \text{m}$.
@step How many groups of $0.5\ \text{m}$ fit in $7.5\ \text{m}$? $7.5 \div 0.5 = 15$
@answer Noor draws the wall $15\ \text{cm}$ long.
:::

Builders and architects often use imperial scales such as $\tfrac14\ \text{in} : 1\ \text{ft}$: every quarter inch on the drawing is one foot on the building.

:::example A deck plan
A deck plan uses the scale $\tfrac14\ \text{in} : 1\ \text{ft}$. The deck is $18\ \text{ft}$ long. How long is it on the plan?
@step Each foot is drawn $\tfrac14\ \text{in}$ long.
@step $18 \times \tfrac14 = 4\tfrac12$
@answer The deck is $4\tfrac12\ \text{in}$ long on the plan.
:::

:::tip Turn a two-unit scale into a ratio
$\tfrac14\ \text{in} : 1\ \text{ft}$ is the same as $\tfrac14\ \text{in} : 12\ \text{in}$. Multiply both parts by $4$ to get $1 : 48$. Real lengths are $48$ times the drawing lengths.
:::

## Finding the scale

:::definition Scale factor
The **scale factor** $k$ tells how many times as long the new figure is as the original:
$$k = \dfrac{\text{new length}}{\text{original length}}$$
If $k > 1$, the new figure is an **enlargement**. If $k < 1$, it is a **reduction**.
:::

To find the scale of a model, write both lengths in the same unit and divide.

:::example A model pickup
A model pickup truck is $24\ \text{cm}$ long. The real truck is $5.76\ \text{m}$ long. What is the scale of the model?
@step Same units: $5.76\ \text{m} = 5.76 \times 100 = 576\ \text{cm}$
@step $576 \div 24 = 24$
@answer The real truck is $24$ times as long as the model, so the scale is $1 : 24$.
:::

A drawing of a small part is often an **enlargement**. On a scale of $2 : 1$, the drawing is twice the real size. A bolt head drawn $38\ \text{mm}$ wide is really $38 \div 2 = 19\ \text{mm}$ wide.

## Area and volume with a scale factor

When every length is multiplied by $k$:
- **area** is multiplied by $k^2$, because both the length and the width grow
- **volume** and **capacity** are multiplied by $k^3$, because the length, width and height all grow

:::

:::example A bigger sign
A sign has an area of $1.2\ \text{m}^2$. A new sign with the same shape has every length $2.5$ times as long. What is its area?
@step Area is multiplied by $k^2 = 2.5^2 = 6.25$.
@step $1.2 \times 6.25 = 7.5$
@answer The new sign has an area of $7.5\ \text{m}^2$.
:::

:::mistake Multiplying area by k
Doubling every length of a patio does **not** double its area: the area is $2^2 = 4$ times as large. A tank with every length doubled holds $2^3 = 8$ times as much.
:::

:::check m203-scale-area-volume 1
:::

:::example A model grain bin
A $1 : 50$ model of a grain bin holds $1600\ \text{cm}^3$. How many cubic metres does the real bin hold?
@step $k = 50$, so volume is multiplied by $k^3 = 50^3 = 125\,000$.
@step $1600 \times 125\,000 = 200\,000\,000\ \text{cm}^3$
@step $1\ \text{m}^3 = 100 \times 100 \times 100 = 1\,000\,000\ \text{cm}^3$, so $200\,000\,000 \div 1\,000\,000 = 200$.
@answer The real bin holds $200\ \text{m}^3$.
:::

You can also work backward. If a larger tank of the same shape holds $27$ times as much, then $k^3 = 27$, so $k = \sqrt[3]{27} = 3$.

:::example Working backward
Two water tanks have the same shape. The larger one holds $3.375$ times as much as the smaller one, which is $1.2\ \text{m}$ tall. How tall is the larger tank?
@step $k^3 = 3.375$, so $k = \sqrt[3]{3.375} = 1.5$.
@step Lengths are multiplied by $k$: $1.2 \times 1.5 = 1.8$
@answer The larger tank is $1.8\ \text{m}$ tall.
:::

:::summary
- A scale compares a drawing length with a real length: in words, as a ratio like $1 : 50$, or with two units like $\tfrac14\ \text{in} : 1\ \text{ft}$.
- Multiply to go from the drawing to real life; divide to go from real life to the drawing. Keep track of units.
- To find a scale, write both lengths in the same unit and divide. The scale factor $k$ is new length $\div$ original length.
- When lengths are multiplied by $k$, area is multiplied by $k^2$ and volume or capacity by $k^3$.
- To find $k$ from an area ratio, take the square root; from a volume ratio, take the cube root.
:::
