How long does it take money to double? You could try year after year in the compound interest formula, but there is a shortcut you can do in your head. Bankers and financial planners use it every day to size up an offer.

## The Rule of 72

:::definition Rule of 72
For an investment that earns compound interest at a rate of $r$ percent per year, the time to **double** is about
$$\text{doubling time (years)} \approx \dfrac{72}{r}$$
Use the rate as a **percent**: for 6%, use $r = 6$.
:::

:::example Doubling time
Nia puts \$3,000 into an investment account that earns 6% per year, compounded annually. About how long will it take to double?
@step Doubling time $\approx 72 \div 6 = 12$
@answer Nia’s \$3,000 will grow to about \$6,000 in about 12 years.
:::

The rule also works backwards. If you know how long you want to wait, divide 72 by the number of years to find the rate you need.

:::example The rate to double
Owen wants an investment to double in 9 years. About what interest rate does he need?
@step Rate $\approx 72 \div 9 = 8$
@answer Owen needs about 8% per year.
:::

:::mistake Using the decimal rate
The Rule of 72 is the one place you use the rate as a percent, not a decimal. With $0.06$ instead of $6$, you get $72 \div 0.06 = 1{,}200$ years, which is clearly wrong. In the interest formulas, use $0.06$; in the Rule of 72, use $6$.
:::

## Doubling again and again

Once you know the doubling time, the money keeps doubling every time that many years go by. Count the doublings, then double that many times.

:::figure

:::

:::example Three doublings
Tanvir puts \$2,000 into a long-term investment that earns 8% per year, compounded annually. Estimate its value in 27 years.
@step Doubling time $\approx 72 \div 8 = 9$ years
@step Number of doublings: $27 \div 9 = 3$
@step \$2,000 → \$4,000 → \$8,000 → \$16,000
@answer The investment will be worth about \$16,000 in 27 years. (The exact value, $2{,}000(1.08)^{27}$, is about \$15,976.12, so the estimate is close.)
:::

When the rate does not divide evenly into 72, divide anyway and round. At 5%, the doubling time is about $72 \div 5 = 14.4$ years.

:::tip Start early
At 6%, money doubles about every 12 years. Someone who invests \$1,000 at age 18 has about \$16,000 at age 66 (four doublings). Someone who waits until 30 has about \$8,000 at 66 (three doublings), from the same \$1,000.
:::

:::

## How good is the estimate?

The Rule of 72 is an estimate. Check it with the compound interest formula, $A = P(1 + r)^t$ for annual compounding.

:::example Checking the rule at 6%
Ayesha invests \$3,000 at 6% per year, compounded annually. The rule says it doubles in 12 years. What is it really worth after 12 years?
@step $A = 3{,}000(1 + 0.06)^{12} \approx 6{,}036.59$
@step Double the principal is \$6,000.
@answer After 12 years the investment is worth \$6,036.59, a little more than double. The estimate is very close.
:::

For everyday rates the money is within a few percent of double, so the rule is good enough to compare options quickly. When you need the exact amount, use the formula.

## Comparing investment offers

To compare two offers, work out what each one is worth at the end of the same time. A higher rate is not always the better deal: how often the interest compounds, and whether it compounds at all, matter too.

:::example Simple or compound?
Kwame has \$10,000 to invest for 8 years. **Offer A** from Foothills Trust pays 4.5% per year simple interest. **Offer B** from Northern Bank pays 4% per year, compounded monthly. Which is better?
@step Offer A: $10{,}000 + 10{,}000 \times 0.045 \times 8 = 13{,}600.00$
@step Offer B: $n = 12$ and $nt = 12 \times 8 = 96$, so $A = 10{,}000\left(1 + \dfrac{0.04}{12}\right)^{96} \approx 13{,}763.95$
@step Difference: $13{,}763.95 - 13{,}600.00 = 163.95$
@answer Offer B is better by \$163.95, even though its rate is lower.
:::

:::mistake Mixing months and years in the exponent
The exponent is the total number of compounding periods, $nt$. For 8 years compounded monthly that is $12 \times 8 = 96$, not $8$ and not $12$. Write down $n$ and $t$ before you start.
:::

:::check m203-rule-of-72 3
:::

:::summary
- Rule of 72: doubling time $\approx 72 \div r$ and rate $\approx 72 \div$ doubling time, with $r$ as a percent.
- To estimate a value far in the future, count the doublings and double that many times.
- The rule is an estimate. The compound interest formula gives the exact value.
- Compare offers by finding the value of each one after the same time. The rate alone does not tell you which is better.
:::
