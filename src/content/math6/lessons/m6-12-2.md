Toss a coin $10$ times. Do you get exactly $5$ heads? Maybe, maybe not. In this lesson you will predict how likely something is, test your prediction with an experiment, and see why more trials give a better answer.

## Outcomes and events

:::definition Outcome and equally likely
An **outcome** is one possible result of an experiment. Outcomes are **equally likely** when each one has the same chance of happening.
:::

- Tossing a coin has $2$ equally likely outcomes: heads and tails.
- Rolling a six-sided die has $6$ equally likely outcomes: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$.
- This spinner has $4$ equal sections, so it has $4$ equally likely outcomes: blue, orange, green and yellow.

:::definition Event
An **event** is one or more outcomes. "Getting an even number" is an event when you roll a die.
:::

:::example Outcomes in an event
You roll a six-sided die. Which outcomes make up the event "a number greater than $4$"?
@step List all the outcomes: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$.
@step Check each one: only $5$ and $6$ are greater than $4$.
@answer The event is made of $2$ outcomes: $5$ and $6$.
:::

## Predicting likelihood

Compare the outcomes in the event with all the equally likely outcomes.

| Outcomes in the event | Likelihood | Die example |
|---|---|---|
| none | impossible | rolling a $7$ |
| fewer than half | unlikely | rolling a $6$ |
| exactly half | equally likely to happen or not happen | rolling an even number |
| more than half | likely | rolling a number greater than $2$ |
| all | certain | rolling a number less than $7$ |

:::example How many times to expect
You roll a die $60$ times. About how many times should you expect to roll a $6$?
@step One of the $6$ equally likely outcomes is a $6$.
@step So expect a $6$ about $1$ out of every $6$ rolls: $60 \div 6 = 10$
@answer About $10$ times. The real result will probably be close to $10$, but not always exactly $10$.
:::

## Relative frequency from an experiment

Each time you do an experiment once, it is called a **trial**. Record how often each outcome happens. Then find its relative frequency:
$$\text{relative frequency} = \dfrac{\text{number of times it happened}}{\text{total number of trials}}$$

:::example Tossing a coin
Hamid tossed a coin $50$ times and got heads $23$ times. What is the relative frequency of heads, as a percentage?
@step Relative frequency $= \dfrac{23}{50}$
@step Multiply the top and bottom by $2$: $\dfrac{23}{50} = \dfrac{46}{100}$
@answer The relative frequency of heads is $46\%$. This is close to half, which is what we predicted.
:::

:::mistake Expecting an exact match
Relative frequency changes from one experiment to the next just by chance. Getting $46\%$ heads instead of $50\%$ does not mean the coin is unfair or that you made a mistake.
:::

## The law of large numbers

:::definition Law of large numbers
The **law of large numbers** says that the more trials you do, the closer the relative frequency usually gets to the expected likelihood. More trials give a better estimate.
:::

| Trials | Heads | Relative frequency of heads |
|---|---|---|
| $10$ | $7$ | $70\%$ |
| $100$ | $46$ | $46\%$ |
| $1000$ | $508$ | $50.8\%$ |

:::example Which estimate is better?
The table shows one class's coin tosses. Which row gives the best estimate of how likely heads is?
@step The expected likelihood is $1$ out of $2$, or $50\%$.
@step With $10$ trials, the result ($70\%$) is far from $50\%$. With $1000$ trials, it is very close ($50.8\%$).
@answer The row with $1000$ trials, because more trials usually give a better estimate.
:::

:::tip Combine your results
If every group in a class does the same experiment, add all the results together. The combined result uses more trials, so it is usually a better estimate.
:::

:::summary
- An outcome is one possible result. An event is one or more outcomes.
- With equally likely outcomes, compare the outcomes in the event with all the outcomes to predict the likelihood.
- Relative frequency from an experiment $=$ times it happened $\div$ number of trials.
- Results vary from one experiment to another just by chance.
- The law of large numbers: more trials usually give a better estimate of the likelihood.
:::
