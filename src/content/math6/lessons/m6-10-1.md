How much space does a box of crayons take up? How much sand fills a sandbox? These questions are about **volume**. In this lesson you will measure volume by counting cubes.

## What is volume?

:::definition Volume
**Volume** is the amount of space that a three-dimensional object takes up.
:::

We measure volume with units that are all the same size, like cubes that fit together with no gaps or overlaps. The units can be sugar cubes, linking cubes or blocks. These are **non-standard units**.

:::figure
{"type":"cubes","heights":[[2,3,1,2]]}
:::

:::example Counting cubes
Liam built this model with linking cubes. Every cube can be seen. What is its volume in cubes?
@step Count each stack: $2$, $3$, $1$ and $2$ cubes.
@step $2 + 3 + 1 + 2 = 8$
@answer The volume is $8$ cubes.
:::

## Cubic centimetres and cubic metres

Everyone can use the same units if we choose **standard units** of volume.

:::definition Cubic centimetre and cubic metre
- A **cubic centimetre** ($1\ \text{cm}^3$) is the volume of a cube that is $1\ \text{cm}$ long, $1\ \text{cm}$ wide and $1\ \text{cm}$ high. A small cube from a set of base-ten blocks is $1\ \text{cm}^3$.
- A **cubic metre** ($1\ \text{m}^3$) is the volume of a cube that is $1\ \text{m}$ long, $1\ \text{m}$ wide and $1\ \text{m}$ high. A few students could sit inside it.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"cubes","heights":[[1]]}
:::

Use cubic centimetres for small objects, like a juice box or a pencil case. Use cubic metres for large spaces, like a classroom or a shipping container.

:::example Choosing a unit
Which unit is best for the volume of a classroom: $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$?
@step A classroom is a very large space.
@step It would take millions of centimetre cubes to fill it.
@answer Use cubic metres ($\text{m}^3$).
:::

:::mistake Square units are for area
$\text{cm}^2$ (square centimetres) measures area, a flat surface. $\text{cm}^3$ (cubic centimetres) measures volume, a space. The little $3$ tells you there are three dimensions: length, width and height.
:::

:::

## Layers, rows and columns

A rectangular prism built from cubes is made of **layers**. Each layer has rows and columns of cubes.

:::figure
{"type":"cubes","heights":[[3,3,3,3],[3,3,3,3]],"showFront":true}
:::

:::example Using layers
Each cube is $1\ \text{cm}^3$, and there are no hidden gaps. Find the volume of the prism.
@step Look at the top layer: $2$ rows of $4$ cubes, so $2 \times 4 = 8$ cubes in each layer.
@step The prism is $3$ cubes high, so there are $3$ layers.
@step $8 \times 3 = 24$
@answer The volume is $24\ \text{cm}^3$.
:::

:::tip No need to count every cube
Volume $=$ (cubes in one layer) $\times$ (number of layers). This is much faster than counting cubes one at a time.
:::

:::check m6-volume-units 2
:::

## Volume stays the same

If you take a model apart and build something new with **all** the same cubes, the volume does not change.

:::figure
{"type":"cubes","heights":[[2,2,2,2,2,2]]}
:::

:::example Rebuilding a prism
Kai built a prism $3$ cubes long, $2$ cubes wide and $2$ cubes high. Then Kai used all the cubes to build a new prism $6$ cubes long and $1$ cube wide, shown above. How high is the new prism?
@step First prism: $3 \times 2 \times 2 = 12$ cubes.
@step One layer of the new prism has $6 \times 1 = 6$ cubes.
@step $12 \div 6 = 2$
@answer The new prism is $2$ cubes high. Its volume is still $12$ cubes.
:::

:::check m6-volume-units 3
:::

:::summary
- **Volume** is the amount of space an object takes up. It is measured in cubic units.
- $1\ \text{cm}^3$ is a cube $1\ \text{cm}$ on each side. $1\ \text{m}^3$ is a cube $1\ \text{m}$ on each side.
- Use $\text{cm}^3$ for small objects and $\text{m}^3$ for large spaces.
- Volume of a prism of cubes $=$ (cubes in one layer) $\times$ (number of layers).
- Rearranging the same cubes does not change the volume.
:::
