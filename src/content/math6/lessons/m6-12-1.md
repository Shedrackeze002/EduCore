In one class, $12$ students walk to school. In another class, $25$ students walk. Does the second class have more walkers for its size? You cannot tell until you know how big each class is. **Relative frequency** makes this kind of comparison fair.

## From frequency to relative frequency

:::definition Relative frequency
The **frequency** of a category is how many times it appears in the data. The **relative frequency** is the frequency as a part of the total:
$$\text{relative frequency} = \dfrac{\text{frequency of the category}}{\text{total number of data values}}$$
:::

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["How they get to school","Frequency"],"rows":[["Walk","9"],["Bus","6"],["Car","5"],["Bike","4"],["Total","24"]]}
:::

:::example Relative frequency as a fraction
A Grade 6 class of $24$ students said how they get to school. What is the relative frequency of Walk?
@step Frequency of Walk: $9$. Total: $24$.
@step Relative frequency $= \dfrac{9}{24}$
@step Write it in lowest terms. Divide the top and bottom by $3$: $\dfrac{9}{24} = \dfrac{3}{8}$
@answer The relative frequency of Walk is $\dfrac{3}{8}$. So $3$ out of every $8$ students in the class walk.
:::

:::tip The parts make a whole
The relative frequencies of all the categories add up to $1$ whole: $\dfrac{9}{24} + \dfrac{6}{24} + \dfrac{5}{24} + \dfrac{4}{24} = \dfrac{24}{24} = 1$.
:::

## Fractions, decimals and percentages

A relative frequency can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage. When the total is a factor of $100$ (such as $20$, $25$ or $50$), make an equivalent fraction with denominator $100$.

:::example Three forms
In a survey of $25$ students, $7$ chose hockey. Write the relative frequency of hockey as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage.
@step Fraction: $\dfrac{7}{25}$ (already in lowest terms)
@step Multiply the top and bottom by $4$: $\dfrac{7}{25} = \dfrac{28}{100}$
@step $28$ hundredths is the decimal $0.28$.
@step $28$ out of $100$ is $28\%$.
@answer $\dfrac{7}{25} = 0.28 = 28\%$
:::

:::mistake Writing the frequency instead
The relative frequency of hockey is not $7$. That is the frequency. Always divide by the total.
:::

:::

## Comparing data sets of different sizes

Relative frequency lets you compare the same category across data sets that have different totals.

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["How they get to school","Ms. Okafor’s class","School club"],"rows":[["Walk","12","25"],["Bus","5","15"],["Car","3","10"],["Total","20","50"]]}
:::

:::example Which group walks more, for its size?
Compare the relative frequency of Walk in the two groups.
@step Ms. Okafor's class: $\dfrac{12}{20} = \dfrac{60}{100} = 60\%$
@step School club: $\dfrac{25}{50} = \dfrac{50}{100} = 50\%$
@step $60\% > 50\%$
@answer Walking is a bigger part of Ms. Okafor's class, even though more club members walk.
:::

:::mistake Comparing only the frequencies
$25$ is more than $12$, but the club is much bigger. Comparing frequencies from groups of different sizes is not fair. Compare the relative frequencies instead.
:::

:::check m6-relative-frequency 3
:::

:::summary
- Relative frequency $=$ frequency $\div$ total. It tells what part of the data is in one category.
- It can be written as a fraction in lowest terms, a decimal or a percentage.
- When the total is a factor of $100$, make an equivalent fraction with denominator $100$.
- The relative frequencies of all the categories add up to $1$ (or $100\%$).
- Use relative frequency, not frequency, to compare data sets of different sizes.
:::
