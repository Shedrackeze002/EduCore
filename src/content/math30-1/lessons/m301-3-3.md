When a division by $x - a$ leaves a remainder of $0$, the binomial $x - a$ divides the polynomial exactly: it is a **factor**. That one idea, together with a short list of values worth testing, lets you factor cubic, quartic and quintic polynomials.

## The factor theorem

:::definition Factor theorem
A polynomial $P(x)$ has a factor $x - a$ if and only if $P(a) = 0$.
:::

**Why it works.** By the remainder theorem, dividing $P(x)$ by $x - a$ leaves the remainder $P(a)$. If $P(a) = 0$, the division statement becomes $P(x) = (x - a)Q(x)$, so $x - a$ is a factor. Going the other way, if $P(x) = (x - a)Q(x)$, then $P(a) = 0 \cdot Q(a) = 0$.

So these four statements say the same thing: $x - a$ is a factor of $P(x)$; $P(a) = 0$; $a$ is a **zero** of $P$; and $x = a$ is a root of $P(x) = 0$ and an $x$-intercept of its graph.

:::example Testing a factor
Is $x - 3$ a factor of $P(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6$? Is $x + 2$?
@step $P(3) = (3)^3 - 4(3)^2 + 3 + 6 = 27 - 36 + 3 + 6 = 0$, so $x - 3$ is a factor.
@step For $x + 2$, use $a = -2$: $P(-2) = (-2)^3 - 4(-2)^2 + (-2) + 6 = -8 - 16 - 2 + 6 = -20$.
@answer $x - 3$ is a factor; $x + 2$ is not, because $P(-2) = -20 \ne 0$.
:::

## The integral zero theorem

Which values of $a$ are worth testing? For a polynomial with integer coefficients, the constant term answers that.

:::definition Integral zero theorem
If $x = b$ is an integral zero of a polynomial $P(x)$ with integer coefficients, then $b$ is a factor of the constant term of $P(x)$.
:::

**Why it works.** If $P(b) = a_nb^n + \dots + a_1b + a_0 = 0$, then $a_0 = -b\left(a_nb^{n-1} + \dots + a_1\right)$. The bracket is an integer, so $b$ divides $a_0$.

:::example Possible integral zeros
List the possible integral zeros of $P(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$, and find one zero.
@step The constant term is $6$, whose factors are $1, 2, 3, 6$.
@step Zeros can be negative too: the possible integral zeros are $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6$.
@step Test $1$ first: $P(1) = 1 - 2 - 5 + 6 = 0$.
@answer Possible integral zeros $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6$; $x = 1$ is a zero, so $x - 1$ is a factor.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the negative factors
The list for a constant term of $6$ is $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6$, not just $1, 2, 3, 6$. And the leading coefficient plays no part in the list of possible integral zeros.
:::

:::tip Quick tests
$P(1)$ is the sum of the coefficients, so it takes seconds to check. Test small values first; they keep the arithmetic easy.
:::

:::

## Factoring completely

To factor a polynomial of degree $3$ or more:

1. Remove any common factor.
2. List the possible integral zeros and test them until $P(a) = 0$.
3. Divide by $x - a$ with synthetic division.
4. Repeat with the quotient until it is a quadratic, then factor the quadratic if it can be factored over the integers.

:::example Factoring a cubic
Factor $P(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$ completely.
@step From the previous example, $P(1) = 0$, so $x - 1$ is a factor.
@step Divide by $x - 1$:
$$\begin{array}{r|rrrr} 1 & 1 & -2 & -5 & 6 \\ & & 1 & -1 & -6 \\ \hline & 1 & -1 & -6 & \boxed{0} \end{array}$$
@step The quotient is $x^2 - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2)$.
@answer $P(x) = (x - 1)(x - 3)(x + 2)$
:::

## Higher degrees, grouping and unknown coefficients

For a quartic, find one zero, divide, and repeat with the cubic quotient. A zero can repeat, so test it again.

:::example Factoring a quartic
Factor $P(x) = x^4 - 5x^3 + 5x^2 + 5x - 6$ completely.
@step $P(1) = 1 - 5 + 5 + 5 - 6 = 0$. Divide by $x - 1$:
$$\begin{array}{r|rrrrr} 1 & 1 & -5 & 5 & 5 & -6 \\ & & 1 & -4 & 1 & 6 \\ \hline & 1 & -4 & 1 & 6 & \boxed{0} \end{array}$$
@step Let $Q(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6$. Test: $Q(1) = 4$, but $Q(-1) = -1 - 4 - 1 + 6 = 0$. Divide by $x + 1$:
$$\begin{array}{r|rrrr} -1 & 1 & -4 & 1 & 6 \\ & & -1 & 5 & -6 \\ \hline & 1 & -5 & 6 & \boxed{0} \end{array}$$
@step The quotient is $x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)$.
@answer $P(x) = (x + 1)(x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)$
:::

Some polynomials factor by **grouping**: $x^3 + 2x^2 - 9x - 18 = x^2(x + 2) - 9(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x^2 - 9) = (x + 2)(x - 3)(x + 3)$.

:::mistake Stopping too early, or going too far
"Completely" means every factor is linear or a quadratic that cannot be factored over the integers. $(x + 2)(x^2 - 9)$ is not finished, because $x^2 - 9$ is a difference of squares. But in $x^3 - x^2 + 3x - 10 = (x - 2)(x^2 + x + 5)$, the quadratic has discriminant $1 - 20 < 0$, so it stays as it is.
:::

The factor theorem can also find an unknown coefficient: make $P(a) = 0$ and solve.

:::example Finding k
Find $k$ so that $x + 2$ is a factor of $P(x) = x^3 + kx^2 - x - 6$.
@step $x + 2$ is a factor exactly when $P(-2) = 0$.
@step $P(-2) = (-2)^3 + k(-2)^2 - (-2) - 6 = -8 + 4k + 2 - 6 = 4k - 12$
@step $4k - 12 = 0$
@answer $k = 3$
:::

:::

:::check m301-poly-factor 3
:::

:::summary
- Factor theorem: $x - a$ is a factor of $P(x)$ exactly when $P(a) = 0$.
- Integral zero theorem: any integral zero is a factor of the constant term, so test $\pm$ each factor.
- To factor completely: common factor, find a zero, divide, repeat, then factor the quadratic. Leave quadratics that cannot be factored over the integers.
- An unknown coefficient can be found by setting $P(a) = 0$.
:::
