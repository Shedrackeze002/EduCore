Checking an equation at a few angles can show that it is **not** an identity, but it can never prove that it is one, any more than testing a few triangles proves the Pythagorean theorem. A proof shows, step by step, that the two sides are equal for every permissible value.

## Verifying numerically and graphically

To verify an identity numerically, substitute a value of $x$ into each side and compare. Choose a value that is permissible, and preferably not a special angle where a coincidence is more likely.

:::example A numerical check
Verify $\sec x - \cos x = \sin x\tan x$ for $x = 30^\circ$.
@step Left side: $\sec 30^\circ - \cos 30^\circ = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} - \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \dfrac{4 - 3}{2\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{6}$
@step Right side: $\sin 30^\circ\tan 30^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{6}$
@answer Both sides equal $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{6} \approx 0.289$, so the identity holds at $x = 30^\circ$.
:::

Graphing both sides is a check at every value at once (within the window). The graphs of $y = \sec x - \cos x$ and $y = \sin x\tan x$ are the same curve, including the vertical asymptotes at the non-permissible values $x = 90^\circ$ and $x = 270^\circ$.

## Strategies for an algebraic proof

Treat the two sides as separate expressions. Work on one side (usually the more complicated one) until it looks exactly like the other side, or simplify each side to the same expression.

1. Rewrite in terms of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$.
2. Use a common denominator, or split a single fraction into two.
3. Replace $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x$, $1 - \sin^2 x$, $1 + \tan^2 x$, $\sin 2x$ or $\cos 2x$ using an identity.
4. Factor, expand, or multiply by a conjugate such as $\dfrac{1 + \cos x}{1 + \cos x}$.

:::example Multiplying by a conjugate
Prove $\dfrac{\sin x}{1 - \cos x} = \dfrac{1 + \cos x}{\sin x}$.
@step Left side: $\dfrac{\sin x}{1 - \cos x}\cdot\dfrac{1 + \cos x}{1 + \cos x} = \dfrac{\sin x(1 + \cos x)}{1 - \cos^2 x}$
@step Pythagorean identity: $= \dfrac{\sin x(1 + \cos x)}{\sin^2 x}$
@step Divide out $\sin x$: $= \dfrac{1 + \cos x}{\sin x}$, which is the right side.
@answer The identity is true for $x \ne \pi n,\ n \in \mathrm{I}$.
:::

:::example A proof with a double angle
Prove $\dfrac{\sin 2x}{1 - \cos 2x} = \cot x$.
@step Left side: $\dfrac{2\sin x\cos x}{1 - (1 - 2\sin^2 x)}$. Choosing $\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$ makes the 1s cancel.
@step $= \dfrac{2\sin x\cos x}{2\sin^2 x} = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}$
@step Quotient identity: $= \cot x$, which is the right side.
@answer The identity is true for $x \ne \pi n,\ n \in \mathrm{I}$, since $1 - \cos 2x = 0$ and $\sin x = 0$ both happen at $x = \pi n$.
:::

:::mistake Treating it as an equation
Do not add the same thing to both sides, cross-multiply, or square both sides: those moves assume the two sides are already equal, which is what you are trying to prove. And never cancel terms: $\dfrac{1 + \sin x}{\sin x}$ is $\csc x + 1$, not $1$.
:::

## Non-permissible values of an identity

An identity only claims equality where **both** sides are defined. List the restrictions from every denominator and every $\tan$, $\sec$, $\cot$ or $\csc$ on either side.

:::example Restrictions from both sides
State the non-permissible values of $\tan x + \cot x = \sec x\csc x$.
@step $\tan x$ and $\sec x$ need $\cos x \ne 0$: $x \ne \frac{\pi}{2} + \pi n$.
@step $\cot x$ and $\csc x$ need $\sin x \ne 0$: $x \ne \pi n$.
@answer Together, $x \ne \dfrac{\pi}{2}n,\ n \in \mathrm{I}$ (every multiple of $90^\circ$).
:::

## Finding the error in a proof

When a "proof" ends at something that does not match, look for the first line that is not equal to the line before it. The usual culprits:

- a missing middle term: $(\sin x + \cos x)^2 = \sin^2 x + 2\sin x\cos x + \cos^2 x$;
- a sign or square lost in a Pythagorean identity: $1 - \sin^2 x = \cos^2 x$;
- the wrong identity: $1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x$, but $1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x$;
- $\sin 2x$ written as $2\sin x$;
- subtracting a fraction without changing the sign of every term in its numerator.

:::tip Test the suspicious line
Substitute a value such as $x = 30^\circ$ into two consecutive lines. If they give different numbers, the error is between them.
:::

:::summary
- A numerical or graphical check supports an identity; one counterexample shows an equation is **not** an identity.
- To prove an identity, work on one side (or each side separately) until both sides match. Never move terms across the equals sign.
- Useful moves: rewrite in sine and cosine, common denominators, Pythagorean and double-angle substitutions, factoring, conjugates.
- An identity holds only where both sides are defined: state its non-permissible values, for all real numbers with $n \in \mathrm{I}$.
:::
