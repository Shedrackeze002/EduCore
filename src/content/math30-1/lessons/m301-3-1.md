A sound engineer shaping a speaker cone, a civil engineer designing the curve of a ramp and a biologist modelling a population all use **polynomial functions**. Before you graph one, learn to read its key features straight from the equation.

## What is a polynomial function?

:::definition Polynomial function
A **polynomial function** of degree $n$ can be written as
$$P(x) = a_nx^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1x + a_0$$
where $n$ is a whole number, the coefficients $a_n, a_{n-1}, \dots, a_0$ are real numbers, and $a_n \ne 0$.
:::

:::definition Degree, leading coefficient and constant term
The **degree** is the greatest exponent of $x$. The **leading coefficient** $a_n$ is the coefficient of that term. The **constant term** $a_0$ is the term with no $x$.
:::

Linear functions (degree $1$) and quadratic functions (degree $2$) are polynomial functions you already know. This unit adds **cubic** (degree $3$), **quartic** (degree $4$) and **quintic** (degree $5$) functions. Functions such as $y = \sqrt{x}$, $y = \dfrac{1}{x}$ and $y = 2^x$ are not polynomial functions, because the exponents on $x$ must be whole numbers.

:::example Reading the features
State the degree, the leading coefficient and the constant term of $f(x) = 3x^4 - 2x^5 + x - 7$.
@step Write the terms in descending order of degree: $f(x) = -2x^5 + 3x^4 + x - 7$.
@step The greatest exponent is $5$, so the degree is $5$ and the leading coefficient is $-2$.
@step The term with no $x$ is $-7$.
@answer Degree $5$, leading coefficient $-2$, constant term $-7$.
:::

:::mistake The first term is not always the leading term
The leading term is the term with the highest power, wherever it is written. In $3x^4 - 2x^5 + x - 7$ the leading coefficient is $-2$, not $3$.
:::

:::check m301-poly-features 1
:::

## End behaviour

:::definition End behaviour
The **end behaviour** of a graph describes what happens to the $y$-values as $x$ moves far to the left ($x \to -\infty$) and far to the right ($x \to \infty$).
:::

For large values of $|x|$, the leading term controls everything. Factor it out:
$$P(x) = a_nx^n\left(1 + \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_nx} + \frac{a_{n-2}}{a_nx^2} + \dots + \frac{a_0}{a_nx^n}\right)$$
As $|x|$ grows, every fraction in the bracket shrinks toward $0$, so the bracket approaches $1$ and $P(x)$ behaves like $a_nx^n$. Only two things matter: whether the degree $n$ is odd or even, and the sign of $a_n$.

| Degree | $a_n > 0$ | $a_n < 0$ |
|---|---|---|
| odd | falls left, rises right: quadrant III to quadrant I | rises left, falls right: quadrant II to quadrant IV |
| even | rises at both ends: quadrant II to quadrant I | falls at both ends: quadrant III to quadrant IV |

## Intercepts and turning points

:::definition Turning point
A **turning point** is a point where the graph changes from increasing to decreasing, or from decreasing to increasing. It is a local maximum or a local minimum.
:::

For a polynomial function of degree $n$:

- the $y$-intercept is $P(0) = a_0$, the constant term;
- there are **at most $n$** $x$-intercepts. An odd-degree graph has **at least one**, because its ends are on opposite sides of the $x$-axis and the graph has no breaks. An even-degree graph may have none;
- there are **at most $n - 1$** turning points.

The cubic $y = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$ has the greatest possible number of $x$-intercepts ($3$) and of turning points ($2$). Its $y$-intercept is its constant term, $6$.

:::example Counting intercepts and turning points
For $f(x) = 3x^4 - 2x^5 + x - 7$, state the possible numbers of $x$-intercepts and the maximum number of turning points.
@step The degree is $5$, which is odd, so the graph has at least $1$ and at most $5$ $x$-intercepts.
@step The maximum number of turning points is $5 - 1 = 4$.
@answer $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$ or $5$ $x$-intercepts; at most $4$ turning points.
:::

:::mistake "At most" does not mean "exactly"
The degree only gives upper limits. A cubic can have three $x$-intercepts, but $y = x^3$ has just one, and it has no turning points at all.
:::

Read the other way, the limits tell you about the degree from a graph. A graph with $t$ turning points has degree at least $t + 1$, and the end behaviour tells you whether that degree is odd or even.

## Matching graphs and equations

To decide whether an equation could belong to a graph, check:

1. the end behaviour: odd or even degree, and the sign of the leading coefficient;
2. the $y$-intercept: the constant term;
3. the $x$-intercepts and turning points: no more than the degree allows.

:::example Which equation?
Which equation could represent the graph of the cubic in the previous section, which extends from quadrant III to quadrant I and passes through $(0, 6)$?

- **A.** $y = -x^3 + 2x^2 + 5x - 6$
- **B.** $y = x^4 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$
- **C.** $y = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x - 6$
- **D.** $y = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$
@step From quadrant III to quadrant I means odd degree and a positive leading coefficient. This rules out A (negative leading coefficient) and B (even degree).
@step The $y$-intercept is $6$, so the constant term is $6$. This rules out C.
@answer D: $y = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$
:::

:::check m301-poly-features 3
:::

:::summary
- The degree is the greatest exponent; the leading coefficient is its coefficient; the constant term is the $y$-intercept.
- End behaviour depends only on the leading term: odd degree means the ends go opposite ways, even degree means the same way; a positive leading coefficient makes the right end rise.
- Degree $n$: at most $n$ $x$-intercepts (at least $1$ if $n$ is odd) and at most $n - 1$ turning points.
- Match a graph to an equation by checking end behaviour first, then the $y$-intercept.
:::
