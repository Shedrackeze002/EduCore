Wrap a number line around a circle of radius $1$ and every angle lands on exactly one point. The coordinates of that point are the cosine and sine of the angle, and they make the rest of trigonometry fit together.

## The equation of the unit circle

The **unit circle** is the circle of radius $1$ centred at the origin. A point $(x, y)$ is on it exactly when its distance from the origin is $1$:
$$\sqrt{(x - 0)^2 + (y - 0)^2} = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x^2 + y^2 = 1$$

:::definition Unit circle
The **unit circle** has centre $(0, 0)$ and radius $1$. Its equation is $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. More generally, the circle with centre $(0, 0)$ and radius $r$ has equation $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$.
:::

:::example Is the point on the circle?
Show that $\left(-\dfrac{3}{5}, \dfrac{4}{5}\right)$ lies on the unit circle.
@step $x^2 + y^2 = \dfrac{9}{25} + \dfrac{16}{25} = \dfrac{25}{25} = 1$
@answer The coordinates satisfy $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, so the point is on the unit circle.
:::

:::example A missing coordinate
The point $\left(x, -\dfrac{5}{13}\right)$ is on the unit circle in quadrant III. Determine $x$.
@step $x^2 = 1 - \left(-\dfrac{5}{13}\right)^2 = 1 - \dfrac{25}{169} = \dfrac{144}{169}$
@step $x = \pm\dfrac{12}{13}$, and $x < 0$ in quadrant III.
@answer $x = -\dfrac{12}{13}$
:::

:::mistake Dropping the sign
Taking a square root gives two values. The quadrant decides which one is correct: in quadrant III both coordinates are negative.
:::

## Points for special angles

Let the terminal arm of $\theta$ meet the unit circle at $P(\theta) = (x, y)$. Because the radius is $1$, the coordinates are exactly $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$.

:::definition P(θ)
$P(\theta)$ is the point where the terminal arm of $\theta$ in standard position meets the unit circle:
$$P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$
:::

The special triangles give the points in quadrant I. For $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, the point lies on $y = x$, so $2x^2 = 1$ and $x = y = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. For $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$, the $30^\circ$–$60^\circ$–$90^\circ$ triangle with hypotenuse $1$ has a short side of $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and a long side of $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

:::example A quadrant III point
Determine the exact coordinates of $P\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right)$.
@step $\dfrac{4\pi}{3}$ is in quadrant III, with reference angle $\dfrac{4\pi}{3} - \pi = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$.
@step $P\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = \left(\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$; in quadrant III both coordinates are negative.
@answer $P\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \left(-\dfrac{1}{2}, -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$
:::

:::example A negative angle
Determine the exact coordinates of $P\left(-\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)$.
@step $-\dfrac{3\pi}{4} + 2\pi = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$: the same terminal arm, in quadrant III.
@step The reference angle is $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, and both coordinates are negative.
@answer $P\left(-\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right) = \left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)$
:::

:::mistake Swapping the coordinates of π/6 and π/3

:::

## Symmetry and larger circles

Reflections and half turns move $P(\theta)$ to points with the same numbers but different signs:

| Angle | Point | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $\theta + \pi$ | $(-x, -y)$ | half a turn |
| $-\theta$ | $(x, -y)$ | reflection in the $x$-axis |
| $\pi - \theta$ | $(-x, y)$ | reflection in the $y$-axis |

:::example Using symmetry
$P(\theta) = \left(\dfrac{5}{13}, \dfrac{12}{13}\right)$. Determine $P(\theta + \pi)$ and $P(-\theta)$.
@step Half a turn changes both signs: $P(\theta + \pi) = \left(-\dfrac{5}{13}, -\dfrac{12}{13}\right)$
@step Reflecting in the $x$-axis changes the sign of $y$: $P(-\theta) = \left(\dfrac{5}{13}, -\dfrac{12}{13}\right)$
@answer $\left(-\dfrac{5}{13}, -\dfrac{12}{13}\right)$ and $\left(\dfrac{5}{13}, -\dfrac{12}{13}\right)$
:::

On a circle of radius $r$, the terminal arm meets the circle at $(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$: the unit-circle point scaled by $r$.

:::example A circle of radius 4
The terminal arm of $\theta = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ meets the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 16$ at $A$. Determine $A$.
@step $r = 4$ and $P\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \left(-\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$.
@step $A = \left(4\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right), 4\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\right)$
@answer $A = \left(-2, 2\sqrt{3}\right)$
:::

:::example From a point to the angle
The terminal arm of $\theta$, $0 \le \theta < 2\pi$, meets the unit circle at $\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)$. Determine $\theta$.
@step $x > 0$ and $y < 0$: quadrant IV. Equal sizes mean the reference angle is $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.
@step $\theta = 2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{4}$
@answer $\theta = \dfrac{7\pi}{4}$
:::

:::summary
- The unit circle is $x^2 + y^2 = 1$; a circle of radius $r$ centred at the origin is $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$.
- $P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is where the terminal arm of $\theta$ meets the unit circle.
- Special angles: $P\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$, $P\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)$, $P\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$.
- In other quadrants, the reference angle gives the numbers and the quadrant gives the signs.
:::
