Interchange $x$ and $y$ in $y = 2^{x}$ and you get $x = 2^{y}$, which in logarithmic form is $y = \log_{2} x$. So every logarithmic function is the inverse of an exponential function, and its graph is a reflection in the line $y = x$.

:::definition Logarithmic function
A function of the form $y = \log_{c} x$, where $c > 0$ and $c \ne 1$. It is the inverse of $y = c^{x}$.
:::

Because the roles of $x$ and $y$ swap, the features of $y = \log_{c} x$ are those of $y = c^{x}$ with $x$ and $y$ interchanged:

| | $y = c^{x}$ | $y = \log_{c} x$ |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | all real numbers | $x > 0$ |
| Range | $y > 0$ | all real numbers |
| Intercept | $y$-intercept $1$ | $x$-intercept $1$ |
| Asymptote | horizontal, $y = 0$ | vertical, $x = 0$ |
| Key point | $(1, c)$ | $(c, 1)$ |

For $c > 1$ both functions are increasing; for $0 < c < 1$ both are decreasing.

:::example Inverse and points
Write the inverse of $y = 5^{x}$ and find three points on its graph.
@step Interchange $x$ and $y$: $x = 5^{y}$, so $y = \log_{5} x$.
@step $(0, 1)$, $(1, 5)$ and $\left(-1, \frac{1}{5}\right)$ are on $y = 5^{x}$, so $(1, 0)$, $(5, 1)$ and $\left(\frac{1}{5}, -1\right)$ are on $y = \log_{5} x$.
@answer $y = \log_{5} x$, through $(1, 0)$, $(5, 1)$ and $\left(\frac{1}{5}, -1\right)$
:::

## Transforming logarithmic functions

The transformations work exactly as for other functions:
$$y = a\log_{c}\left(b(x - h)\right) + k \qquad (x, y) \to \left(\frac{x}{b} + h,\ ay + k\right)$$

Only $h$ moves the vertical asymptote: it becomes $x = h$. The domain is $x > h$ when $b > 0$ and $x < h$ when $b < 0$ (a reflection in the $y$-axis). The range stays all real numbers.

:::example Graph a transformed logarithm
For $y = 2\log_{3}(x + 1) - 4$, state the asymptote and domain, and find the images of $(1, 0)$, $(3, 1)$ and $(9, 2)$ from $y = \log_{3} x$.
@step $a = 2$, $b = 1$, $h = -1$, $k = -4$. Asymptote $x = -1$; domain $\{x \mid x > -1,\ x \in \mathbb{R}\}$.
@step Mapping $(x, y) \to (x - 1,\ 2y - 4)$: $(1, 0) \to (0, -4)$, $(3, 1) \to (2, -2)$ and $(9, 2) \to (8, 0)$.
@answer Asymptote $x = -1$; domain $x > -1$; image points $(0, -4)$, $(2, -2)$ and $(8, 0)$
:::

## Intercepts, domains and inverses

To find an $x$-intercept, set $y = 0$, isolate the logarithm and convert to exponential form.

:::example An x-intercept
Find the $x$-intercept of $y = 2\log_{3}(x + 1) - 4$.
@step $0 = 2\log_{3}(x + 1) - 4$, so $\log_{3}(x + 1) = 2$.
@step $x + 1 = 3^{2} = 9$
@answer $x = 8$, which matches the point $(8, 0)$ on the graph above.
:::

:::example A domain from a linear argument
State the domain of $y = \log(6 - 2x)$.
@step The argument must be positive: $6 - 2x > 0$.
@step $-2x > -6$. Dividing by $-2$ reverses the inequality: $x < 3$.
@answer $\{x \mid x < 3,\ x \in \mathbb{R}\}$
:::

:::example Inverse of a transformed function
Find the inverse of $y = 2^{x - 3} + 1$.
@step Interchange $x$ and $y$: $x = 2^{y - 3} + 1$.
@step Isolate the power: $x - 1 = 2^{y - 3}$.
@step Logarithmic form: $y - 3 = \log_{2}(x - 1)$.
@answer $y = \log_{2}(x - 1) + 3$. The horizontal asymptote $y = 1$ of the original becomes the vertical asymptote $x = 1$ of the inverse.
:::

:::summary
- $y = \log_{c} x$ is the inverse of $y = c^{x}$: reflect in $y = x$.
- $y = \log_{c} x$: domain $x > 0$, range all real numbers, $x$-intercept $1$, vertical asymptote $x = 0$, passes through $(c, 1)$.
- For $y = a\log_{c}(b(x - h)) + k$: asymptote $x = h$; domain $x > h$ if $b > 0$, $x < h$ if $b < 0$; mapping $(x, y) \to \left(\frac{x}{b} + h,\ ay + k\right)$.
- To find an $x$-intercept, set $y = 0$ and convert to exponential form.
:::
