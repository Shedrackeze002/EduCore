A wind turbine blade, a bicycle wheel and a satellite in orbit all turn through angles far larger than $360^\circ$. Degrees split a turn into $360$ parts because people chose that number; **radians** measure an angle with the circle itself, which is why calculus and physics use them.

:::definition Radian
One **radian** is the measure of a central angle that subtends an arc equal in length to the radius of the circle.
:::

A full circle has circumference $2\pi r$, so it contains $\dfrac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi$ radians. That gives the key facts:
$$2\pi = 360^\circ \qquad \pi = 180^\circ \qquad 1 \text{ radian} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.3^\circ$$

To convert degrees to radians, multiply by $\dfrac{\pi}{180^\circ}$. To convert radians to degrees, multiply by $\dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi}$. Exact radian measures are written as multiples of $\pi$, and the word "radians" is usually left out: an angle written without a degree sign is in radians.

| Degrees | $30^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ | $180^\circ$ | $270^\circ$ | $360^\circ$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radians | $\frac{\pi}{6}$ | $\frac{\pi}{4}$ | $\frac{\pi}{3}$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $\pi$ | $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ | $2\pi$ |

:::example Degrees to radians
Convert $225^\circ$ to radians. Give an exact answer.
@step $225^\circ \times \dfrac{\pi}{180^\circ} = \dfrac{225\pi}{180}$
@step Divide the numerator and denominator by $45$: $\dfrac{225\pi}{180} = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$
@answer $225^\circ = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$
:::

:::example Radians to degrees
Convert $\dfrac{7\pi}{6}$ to degrees, and convert $2.5$ radians to degrees to the nearest tenth of a degree.
@step $\dfrac{7\pi}{6} \times \dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi} = \dfrac{7(180^\circ)}{6} = 210^\circ$
@step $2.5 \times \dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 143.24^\circ$
@answer $\dfrac{7\pi}{6} = 210^\circ$ and $2.5 \approx 143.2^\circ$
:::

:::mistake Upside-down conversion factor
To go **to** radians the factor has $\pi$ on top: $\dfrac{\pi}{180^\circ}$. A quick check: $180^\circ$ must become $\pi$, not $\dfrac{180^2}{\pi}$.
:::

## Coterminal angles

An angle is in **standard position** when its vertex is at the origin and its initial arm lies along the positive $x$-axis. A positive angle turns counterclockwise; a negative angle turns clockwise.

:::definition Coterminal angles
Angles in standard position are **coterminal** when they share the same terminal arm. They differ by a whole number of full turns, so every angle coterminal with $\theta$ is
$$\theta + 360^\circ n \quad \text{or} \quad \theta + 2\pi n, \qquad n \in \mathbb{I}$$
This is the **general form**.
:::

Turning $240^\circ$ clockwise ends on the same arm as turning $120^\circ$ counterclockwise: $-240^\circ + 360^\circ = 120^\circ$.

:::example A positive and a negative coterminal angle
Determine one positive and one negative angle coterminal with $140^\circ$.
@step Add a full turn: $140^\circ + 360^\circ = 500^\circ$
@step Subtract a full turn: $140^\circ - 360^\circ = -220^\circ$
@answer $500^\circ$ and $-220^\circ$ (any $140^\circ + 360^\circ n$ works)
:::

:::example Radians and the general form
Find the angle between $0$ and $2\pi$ that is coterminal with $\dfrac{17\pi}{4}$, and write the general form of all angles coterminal with it.
@step Write $2\pi = \dfrac{8\pi}{4}$ and subtract it twice: $\dfrac{17\pi}{4} - \dfrac{16\pi}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$
@step Every coterminal angle differs from $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ by a multiple of $2\pi$.
@answer $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$; all coterminal angles are $\dfrac{\pi}{4} + 2\pi n$, $n \in \mathbb{I}$
:::

## Arc length

An arc is a fraction of the whole circle: the fraction is $\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}$ when the central angle $\theta$ is in radians. So the arc length is
$$a = \frac{\theta}{2\pi} \times 2\pi r = r\theta$$

:::definition Arc length
In a circle of radius $r$, a central angle of $\theta$ **radians** subtends an arc of length $a = r\theta$. The arc and the radius are measured in the same unit.
:::

:::example Using a = rθ
A circle has a radius of $8$ cm. Determine the length of the arc subtended by a central angle of $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$, to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.
@step $a = r\theta = 8 \times \dfrac{3\pi}{4} = 6\pi$
@step $6\pi \approx 18.85$
@answer The arc is about $18.8$ cm long.
:::

:::example Angle in degrees
A pendulum $75$ cm long swings through $40^\circ$. How far does its tip travel, to the nearest tenth of a centimetre?
@step Convert: $40^\circ = 40 \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{2\pi}{9}$
@step $a = r\theta = 75 \times \dfrac{2\pi}{9} = \dfrac{50\pi}{3} \approx 52.36$
@answer About $52.4$ cm
:::

:::example Finding the angle
An arc $15$ m long lies on a circle of radius $6$ m. Find the central angle in radians and in degrees.
@step $\theta = \dfrac{a}{r} = \dfrac{15}{6} = 2.5$ radians
@step $2.5 \times \dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 143.2^\circ$
@answer $\theta = 2.5$ radians, or about $143.2^\circ$
:::

:::mistake Degrees in a = rθ
$a = 75 \times 40 = 3000$ cm is far too long for a pendulum. The formula only works with the angle in radians.
:::

:::tip Sector area
The same fraction-of-the-circle reasoning gives the area of a sector: $A = \dfrac{\theta}{2\pi} \times \pi r^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}r^2\theta$, again with $\theta$ in radians.
:::

## Rotations in radians

Radians make turning easy to count: one revolution is $2\pi$ radians.

:::example A spinning wheel
A wheel turns at $45$ revolutions per minute. Through what angle does it turn in $4$ seconds?
@step In $4$ s it makes $45 \times \dfrac{4}{60} = 3$ revolutions.
@step $3 \times 2\pi = 6\pi$ radians, or $3 \times 360^\circ = 1080^\circ$
@answer $6\pi$ radians ($1080^\circ$)
:::

:::check m301-radian-convert 3
:::

:::summary
- One radian subtends an arc equal to the radius; $\pi = 180^\circ$.
- Degrees to radians: multiply by $\dfrac{\pi}{180^\circ}$. Radians to degrees: multiply by $\dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi}$.
- Coterminal angles share a terminal arm: $\theta + 360^\circ n$ or $\theta + 2\pi n$, $n \in \mathbb{I}$.
- Arc length is $a = r\theta$ with $\theta$ in radians.
:::
