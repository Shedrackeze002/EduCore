A committee, a hand of cards and a set of pizza toppings have one thing in common: the order in which the items are chosen does not matter. Counting selections like these needs a new tool, the combination.

## When order does not matter

List the ways to choose 2 of the 4 letters A, B, C and D. As permutations there are ${}_{4}P_{2} = 12$: AB, BA, AC, CA, AD, DA, BC, CB, BD, DB, CD, DC. But as a *selection*, AB and BA are the same pair. Each pair appears $2! = 2$ times, so there are $12 \div 2 = 6$ selections.

In general, each selection of $r$ objects can be arranged in $r!$ orders, so dividing ${}_{n}P_{r}$ by $r!$ counts the selections.

:::definition Combination
A **combination** is a selection of objects in which order does not matter. The number of combinations of $n$ different objects taken $r$ at a time is
$${}_{n}C_{r} = \frac{{}_{n}P_{r}}{r!} = \frac{n!}{(n - r)!\,r!}, \qquad 0 \le r \le n$$
It is also written $\dbinom{n}{r}$.
:::

:::example A selection of lab activities
A physics teacher will choose 3 of 10 lab activities for a unit. How many selections are possible?
@step Only the group matters, not the order, so use ${}_{10}C_{3}$.
@step ${}_{10}C_{3} = \dfrac{10!}{7!\,3!} = \dfrac{10 \times 9 \times 8}{3 \times 2 \times 1}$
@answer $120$ selections.
:::

:::tip Choosing r is the same as leaving out n − r
Every choice of 5 people to go on a trip is also a choice of the 7 who stay home, so ${}_{12}C_{5} = {}_{12}C_{7} = 792$. In general ${}_{n}C_{r} = {}_{n}C_{n - r}$.
:::

## Card hands and other selections

Many problems use a standard deck of 52 cards. It has four suits: hearts and diamonds are red, clubs and spades are black. Each suit has 13 cards: A, 2 to 10, J, Q and K. The J, Q and K are the face cards, so there are 12 face cards in all. A **hand** is a combination, because the order the cards are dealt does not matter.

:::example Hands from one group
How many 5-card hands contain only hearts?
@step Choose all 5 cards from the 13 hearts.
@step ${}_{13}C_{5} = \dfrac{13 \times 12 \times 11 \times 10 \times 9}{5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1}$
@answer $1287$ hands. (Compare: there are ${}_{52}C_{5} = 2\,598\,960$ hands in all.)
:::

When a selection is made from two groups at once, choose from each group and multiply.

:::example Two kings and three queens
How many 5-card hands contain exactly two kings and three queens?
@step Kings: ${}_{4}C_{2} = 6$. Queens: ${}_{4}C_{3} = 4$.
@answer $6 \times 4 = 24$ hands.
:::

## Solving equations with combinations

:::example How many people shook hands?
At a meeting, everyone shook hands with everyone else exactly once. There were 45 handshakes. How many people were there?
@step A handshake is a pair of people, so the number of handshakes is ${}_{n}C_{2} = \dfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}$.
@step $\dfrac{n(n - 1)}{2} = 45$, so $n^2 - n - 90 = 0$ and $(n - 10)(n + 9) = 0$.
@answer $n = 10$ people (reject $n = -9$).
:::

## Cases: exactly, at least and at most

Conditions on a selection usually lead to cases.

- **Exactly:** choose the required number from each group and multiply.
- **At least / at most:** add the cases, or use the **complement**: count everything, then subtract what you do not want.

:::example A committee with conditions
A 4-person committee is chosen from 5 women and 6 men. How many committees have (a) exactly two women, (b) at least one woman?
@step (a) ${}_{5}C_{2} \times {}_{6}C_{2} = 10 \times 15 = 150$
@step (b) All committees: ${}_{11}C_{4} = 330$. Committees with no women: ${}_{6}C_{4} = 15$.
@step At least one woman: $330 - 15 = 315$
@answer (a) $150$; (b) $315$. (Check (b) with cases: $5 \times 20 + 10 \times 15 + 10 \times 6 + 5 = 315$.)
:::

:::mistake Counting "at least one" by placing one person first
It is tempting to choose one woman ($5$ ways) and then any 3 of the other 10 people (${}_{10}C_{3} = 120$ ways), giving $600$. That counts a committee with two women twice (once for each woman chosen "first"), so it is too large. Use cases or the complement instead.
:::

:::summary
- In a combination, order does not matter: ${}_{n}C_{r} = \dfrac{n!}{(n - r)!\,r!} = \dfrac{{}_{n}P_{r}}{r!}$.
- ${}_{n}C_{r} = {}_{n}C_{n - r}$: choosing what to take is choosing what to leave.
- For selections from two groups, choose from each and multiply.
- "Exactly": one case. "At least" or "at most": add the cases, or subtract the unwanted ones from the total.
- Equations such as ${}_{n}C_{2} = 45$ become quadratics; keep only the natural-number root.
:::
