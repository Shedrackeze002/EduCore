A hiking map fits a whole provincial park on one sheet of paper. A floor plan fits a whole house on a page. Both are scale diagrams, and the **scale** tells you how to turn a length on the paper into a real length.

## Reading a scale

:::definition Scale
The **scale** of a diagram or map compares a length on the drawing with the real length. It can be written in words, such as "$1\ \text{cm}$ represents $2\ \text{m}$", or as a ratio, such as $1 : 200$.
:::

Here is a scale diagram of a classroom floor. On this diagram, $1\ \text{cm}$ represents $2\ \text{m}$.

:::example The real classroom
How long and how wide is the real classroom?
@step Each centimetre on the diagram stands for $2\ \text{m}$.

## Scales written as ratios

:::definition Ratio scale
A scale of $1 : 50$ means $1$ unit on the drawing stands for $50$ of the **same** units in real life. So $1\ \text{cm}$ on the plan is $50\ \text{cm}$ in real life.
:::

Because both numbers use the same unit, you usually need to change units at the end. Remember: $1\ \text{m} = 100\ \text{cm}$ and $1\ \text{km} = 100\,000\ \text{cm}$.

:::example A floor plan at 1 : 50
On a plan drawn to a scale of $1 : 50$, a wall is $14.4\ \text{cm}$ long. How long is the real wall?
@step Real length $= 14.4 \times 50 = 720\ \text{cm}$
@step $720\ \text{cm} \div 100 = 7.2\ \text{m}$
@answer The real wall is $7.2\ \text{m}$ long.
:::

:::example Drawing a wall at 1 : 40
Ayesha draws a $6\ \text{m}$ wall on a plan with a scale of $1 : 40$. How long is the wall on her plan?
@step Change to centimetres: $6\ \text{m} = 600\ \text{cm}$.
@step Divide by the scale: $600 \div 40 = 15$
@answer The wall is $15\ \text{cm}$ long on the plan.
:::

## Map scales

Maps of large areas use large scales such as $1 : 50\,000$. Then $1\ \text{cm}$ on the map is $50\,000\ \text{cm}$, or $0.5\ \text{km}$, on the ground.

:::example From the map to the trail
On a map with a scale of $1 : 50\,000$, the trail from the campground to the lookout is $7.4\ \text{cm}$ long. How long is the real trail?
@step Real distance $= 7.4 \times 50\,000 = 370\,000\ \text{cm}$
@step $370\,000 \div 100\,000 = 3.7$
@answer The trail is $3.7\ \text{km}$ long.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting to change units
$7.4 \times 50\,000 = 370\,000$ is in **centimetres**, not kilometres. A hiking trail $370\,000\ \text{km}$ long makes no sense. Always ask: what unit is my answer in?
:::

## Finding the scale and solving problems

If you know a real length and its length on the drawing, you can find the scale. Put both in the same unit and divide: real $\div$ drawing.

:::example The scale of a gym plan
A school gym is $30\ \text{m}$ long. On a plan, it is $15\ \text{cm}$ long. What is the scale?
@step $30\ \text{m} = 3000\ \text{cm}$
@step $3000 \div 15 = 200$
@answer The scale is $1 : 200$.
:::

:::example Fencing a garden
A plan of a garden uses a scale of $1 : 200$. The garden is a rectangle $9.5\ \text{cm}$ by $6\ \text{cm}$ on the plan. How much fencing goes around the real garden?
@step Length: $9.5 \times 200 = 1900\ \text{cm} = 19\ \text{m}$
@step Width: $6 \times 200 = 1200\ \text{cm} = 12\ \text{m}$
@step Perimeter: $2 \times 19 + 2 \times 12 = 62$
@answer The garden needs $62\ \text{m}$ of fencing.
:::

:::tip Check that the answer makes sense
A garden plan should give a garden a few metres long, not a few kilometres. A quick estimate catches most unit mistakes.
:::

:::summary
- A scale such as "$1\ \text{cm}$ represents $2\ \text{m}$" or $1 : 200$ links lengths on a drawing to real lengths.
- In a ratio scale $1 : n$, both parts use the same unit: real length $= n \times$ drawing length.
- Drawing length $=$ real length $\div n$ (change to the same unit first).
- $1\ \text{m} = 100\ \text{cm}$ and $1\ \text{km} = 100\,000\ \text{cm}$.
- To find the scale, divide the real length by the drawing length, in the same unit.
:::
