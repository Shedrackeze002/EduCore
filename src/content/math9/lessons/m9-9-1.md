Logos, quilt blocks, Indigenous beadwork, snowflakes and road signs often look balanced. Much of that balance comes from line symmetry: one half is the mirror image of the other.

## Lines of symmetry

:::definition Line symmetry
A shape has **line symmetry** when a line divides it into two halves that are mirror images. That line is a **line of symmetry**: if you fold along it, the two halves match exactly.
:::

A rectangle has $2$ lines of symmetry: one up and down, and one across.

:::mistake The diagonal of a rectangle
A diagonal cuts a rectangle into two equal pieces, but it is **not** a line of symmetry. If you fold along it, the corners do not match.

:::tip Regular polygons
A regular polygon with $n$ sides has $n$ lines of symmetry. An equilateral triangle has $3$, a square has $4$ and a regular hexagon has $6$.
:::

## Completing a shape

If you know a line of symmetry and half of a shape, you can draw the other half. Each missing point is the **mirror image** of a point you have: it is the same distance from the line, on the other side.

The shape below has the line $x = 1$ as a line of symmetry. Only half is drawn.

:::example The image of C
Find $C'$, the image of $C(-4, 0)$ in the line $x = 1$.
@step $C$ is $1 - (-4) = 5$ units left of the line.
@step $C'$ is $5$ units right of the line: $x = 1 + 5 = 6$. The $y$-coordinate does not change.
@answer $C' = (6, 0)$
:::

In the same way, $B(-2, -3)$ goes to $B'(4, -3)$ and $D(-1, 2)$ goes to $D'(3, 2)$. Joining the images completes the shape.

## Slanted lines and whole designs

Reflecting in the line $y = x$ swaps the two coordinates. The image of $(2, -4)$ is $(-4, 2)$. Reflecting in $y = -x$ swaps them and changes both signs: $(2, -4)$ goes to $(4, -2)$.

For a design with several parts, a line of symmetry must work for **every** part, and colours must match too. This flower decal has $6$ lines of symmetry: $3$ through opposite petals and $3$ between them.

:::summary
- A line of symmetry is a fold line where the two halves match exactly.
- A regular polygon with $n$ sides has $n$ lines of symmetry. A diagonal of a rectangle is not a line of symmetry.
- To complete a shape, put each image point the same distance from the line of symmetry, on the other side.
- Reflecting in $y = x$ swaps the coordinates.
- In a design, a line of symmetry must work for every part and every colour.
:::
