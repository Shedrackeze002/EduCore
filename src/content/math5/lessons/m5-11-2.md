How many legs do $25$ spiders have? You could count by eights $25$ times, but there is a faster way. A rule that connects each position to its term lets you jump straight to any term.

## A rule from position to term

A **rule** tells you how to get a term from its position. In this unit, the rule uses just **one operation**, such as "multiply the position by $4$" or "add $6$ to the position".

:::figure
{"type":"table","horizontal":true,"headers":["Position","Term"],"rows":[["1","4"],["2","8"],["3","12"],["4","16"]]}
:::

:::example Finding the rule
Find a rule for the table above.
@step Compare each term with its own position: $4 = 4 \times 1$, $8 = 4 \times 2$, $12 = 4 \times 3$, $16 = 4 \times 4$.
@step Every term is its position multiplied by $4$.
@answer The rule is $\text{term} = 4 \times \text{position}$.
:::

Here is another table: positions $1, 2, 3, 4$ and terms $7, 8, 9, 10$. Each term is $6$ more than its position, so $\text{term} = \text{position} + 6$.

:::mistake Looking only at the change
In the first table, the terms go up by $4$, so it is tempting to write "$\text{term} = \text{position} + 4$". Test it: position $2$ gives $2 + 4 = 6$, but the term is $8$. Always compare each term with its **own position**, and test your rule on every column.
:::

## Writing an expression

:::definition Variable and expression
A **variable** is a letter that stands for a number. We use $n$ for the position. An **expression** such as $4n$ or $n + 6$ tells how to get the term at position $n$.
:::

$4n$ means $4 \times n$. We write the number first and leave out the $\times$ sign.

:::example From a rule to an expression
Write an expression for each rule: (a) $\text{term} = 9 \times \text{position}$ (b) $\text{term} = \text{position} + 12$
@step Replace the word "position" with $n$.
@step (a) $9 \times n$ is written $9n$.
@step (b) $n + 12$
@answer (a) $9n$ (b) $n + 12$
:::

:::check m5-sequence-rule 2
:::

## Finding any term

Once you know the rule, you can find the term at any position. Put the position in place of $n$.

:::example The term at position 25
The expression $4n$ gives the term at position $n$. What is the term at position $25$?
@step Replace $n$ with $25$: $4 \times 25$
@step $4 \times 25 = 100$
@answer The term at position $25$ is $100$.
:::

:::example Using a table
The table shows the terms $11, 12, 13, 14$ at positions $1, 2, 3, 4$. What is the term at position $30$?
@step Compare: $11 = 1 + 10$, $12 = 2 + 10$, $13 = 3 + 10$. The rule is $n + 10$.
@step Position $30$: $30 + 10 = 40$
@answer The term at position $30$ is $40$.
:::

## Solving problems with sequences

A spider has $8$ legs. One spider has $8$ legs, two spiders have $16$, three have $24$. The number of spiders is the position, and the number of legs is the term. The expression is $8n$.

:::example Working forward and backward
(a) How many legs do $15$ spiders have? (b) How many spiders have $96$ legs in all?
@step (a) Use the rule with $n = 15$: $8 \times 15 = 120$
@step (b) Now you know the term, $96$. Work backwards: undo "multiply by $8$" by dividing by $8$.
@step $96 \div 8 = 12$. Check: $8 \times 12 = 96$ ✓
@answer (a) $15$ spiders have $120$ legs. (b) $12$ spiders have $96$ legs.
:::

:::tip Work backwards
If the rule multiplies, divide to go back. If the rule adds, subtract to go back.
:::

:::summary
- A rule connects each position to its term with one operation, such as $\text{term} = 4 \times \text{position}$.
- Find the rule by comparing each term with its own position. Test it on every column.
- Write the rule as an expression with $n$ for the position: $4n$ or $n + 6$.
- To find any term, put its position in place of $n$.
- To find the position of a term, work backwards: divide or subtract.
:::
