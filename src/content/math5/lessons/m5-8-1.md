Fold a paper heart down the middle and the two halves match. Spin a pinwheel and it looks the same again and again. Both of these are **symmetry**, and you can find it in nature, in art and in designs all around you.

## Lines of symmetry

:::definition Line of symmetry
A 2-D shape has **reflection symmetry** if you can fold it along a straight line and the two halves match exactly. That fold line is called a **line of symmetry**.
:::

A shape can have no lines of symmetry, one line, or many lines. This rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry: one across and one up and down.

:::mistake The diagonal of a rectangle
A diagonal cuts a rectangle into two pieces that are the same size. But it is **not** a line of symmetry. If you fold along it, the corners do not land on each other.
:::

:::example An arrow sign
How many lines of symmetry does this arrow sign have?
@step Fold it along a line across the middle, from the tail to the tip. The top half lands exactly on the bottom half.
@step Fold it up and down. The tip lands on the tail, and they do not match.
@step A slanted fold does not work either.

## Rotation symmetry

Some shapes fit on themselves when you **turn** them around their centre.

:::definition Rotation symmetry
A 2-D shape has **rotation symmetry** if it fits exactly on itself after a turn of less than a full turn ($360^\circ$) around its centre point.
:::

:::definition Order of rotation symmetry
The **order of rotation symmetry** is the number of times a shape fits exactly on itself during one full turn. The full turn counts. A shape with order $1$ only fits after a full turn, so it has **no** rotation symmetry.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"plane","grid":{"xMin":0,"xMax":340,"yMin":0,"yMax":215,"step":340},"hideTickLabels":true,"polylines":[{"points":[[170,107.2],[269.2,107.2],[269.2,173.33],[170,107.2]],"color":"brand","fill":true},{"points":[[170,107.2],[170,206.4],[103.87,206.4],[170,107.2]],"color":"brand","fill":true},{"points":[[170,107.2],[70.8,107.2],[70.8,41.07],[170,107.2]],"color":"brand","fill":true},{"points":[[170,107.2],[170,8],[236.13,8],[170,107.2]],"color":"brand","fill":true}],"caption":"A pinwheel with four blades"}
:::

:::example A pinwheel
What is the order of rotation symmetry of this pinwheel?
@step Trace the pinwheel. Turn the tracing a quarter turn ($90^\circ$). It fits exactly. That is $1$.
@step It fits again after $180^\circ$ and after $270^\circ$. That is $2$ and $3$.
@step It fits again after the full turn ($360^\circ$). That is $4$.
@answer The order of rotation symmetry is $4$.
:::

The pinwheel has no lines of symmetry, but it does have rotation symmetry. The two kinds of symmetry are different.

:::tip Use tracing paper
Trace the shape and put a pencil point on its centre. Turn the tracing slowly and count each time it fits on the shape. Stop when you get back to the start.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the full turn
Every shape fits on itself after a full turn, so the order is never $0$. A shape that only fits after a full turn has order $1$.
:::

:::check m5-symmetry 2
:::

## Central symmetry: the half turn

:::definition Central symmetry
A shape has **central symmetry** if it fits exactly on itself after a **half turn** ($180^\circ$) around its centre. Central symmetry is rotation symmetry by $180^\circ$.
:::

In a half turn, every point moves along a straight line through the centre. It stops the same distance from the centre, on the other side.

:::figure

:::example A half turn of a parallelogram
The parallelogram has central symmetry about its centre $O$. After a half turn, which vertex lands where vertex $A$ was?
@step Draw a straight line from $A$ through $O$.
@step Keep going the same distance past $O$. The line reaches vertex $C$.
@answer Vertex $C$ lands where $A$ was. (And $B$ and $D$ trade places too.)
:::

:::tip Even orders
A shape with order of rotation symmetry $2$, $4$, $6$ or $8$ fits after a half turn, so it has central symmetry.
:::

:::

## Symmetry in nature and in designs

Symmetry happens in nature. A butterfly has a line of symmetry down its middle. A sea star (starfish) with 5 arms has 5 lines of symmetry. A snowflake usually has 6 arms, so it has rotation symmetry.

People also create symmetry. You can find it in First Nations, Métis and Inuit designs such as beadwork, basket weaving, quilts and wampum belts, and in buildings such as tipis and longhouses. A tipi seen from the front has a line of symmetry down the middle. Many star blankets have a large eight-pointed star in the centre.

:::example A quilt block
How many lines of symmetry does this quilt block have, and what is its order of rotation symmetry?
@step The square and the diamond both match along the vertical line, the horizontal line and the two diagonals: $4$ lines.
@step A quarter turn fits the whole block on itself, colours too. It fits $4$ times in a full turn.
@answer $4$ lines of symmetry and an order of rotation symmetry of $4$.
:::

:::note 3-D shapes
3-D shapes can have symmetry too. A 3-D shape has reflection symmetry if a flat cut (a plane) splits it into two halves that are mirror images. A can of soup cut straight down the middle is one example.
:::

:::summary
- A line of symmetry is a fold line where the two halves match exactly.
- A shape has rotation symmetry if it fits on itself after a turn of less than $360^\circ$.
- The order of rotation symmetry counts how many times a shape fits on itself in one full turn. Order $1$ means no rotation symmetry.
- Central symmetry is rotation symmetry by a half turn ($180^\circ$). Each point moves along a straight line through the centre.
- Symmetry is found in nature and in many designs, including First Nations, Métis and Inuit designs.
:::
