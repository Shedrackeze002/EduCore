Liam's family orders two pizzas. Each pizza is cut into $4$ equal slices. They eat $7$ slices. That is more than one whole pizza! In this lesson you will write amounts greater than $1$ with fractions.

## Fractions greater than 1

Count the slices by fourths: $\dfrac{1}{4}, \dfrac{2}{4}, \dfrac{3}{4}, \dfrac{4}{4}$. Four fourths make one whole pizza. Keep counting: $\dfrac{5}{4}, \dfrac{6}{4}, \dfrac{7}{4}$.

:::definition Improper fraction
An **improper fraction** has a numerator (top) that is greater than its denominator (bottom), such as $\dfrac{7}{4}$ or $\dfrac{5}{3}$. It is more than $1$ whole.
:::

A natural number can be written as a fraction too. With denominator $1$, each whole is one piece: $3 = \dfrac{3}{1}$. With fourths, each whole is $4$ pieces: $2 = \dfrac{8}{4}$.

:::example Counting by thirds
Count by thirds from $\dfrac{1}{3}$. What comes after $\dfrac{5}{3}$?
@step Each step adds one more $\dfrac{1}{3}$, so the numerator goes up by $1$.
@step $\dfrac{1}{3}, \dfrac{2}{3}, \dfrac{3}{3}, \dfrac{4}{3}, \dfrac{5}{3}, \dfrac{6}{3}$
@answer $\dfrac{6}{3}$ comes next. It is $2$ wholes.
:::

## Mixed numbers

Look at the picture again. One bar is full, and $3$ more fourths are shaded. We can write that as $1\dfrac{3}{4}$. We read it "one and three fourths".

:::definition Mixed number
A **mixed number** has a whole-number part and a fraction part, such as $1\dfrac{3}{4}$. It means $1 + \dfrac{3}{4}$.
:::

To change an improper fraction to a mixed number, **divide** the numerator by the denominator.

:::example From an improper fraction to a mixed number
Write $\dfrac{11}{4}$ as a mixed number.
@step Every $4$ fourths make $1$ whole. Divide: $11 \div 4 = 2$ R $3$.
@step The quotient $2$ is the number of wholes.
@step The remainder $3$ is the number of fourths left over: $\dfrac{3}{4}$.
@answer $\dfrac{11}{4} = 2\dfrac{3}{4}$
:::

:::mistake Keep the denominator
The pieces are still fourths, so the fraction part has denominator $4$. Do not use the quotient or the remainder as the denominator. $\dfrac{11}{4}$ is $2\dfrac{3}{4}$, not $2\dfrac{3}{2}$ or $3\dfrac{2}{4}$.
:::

:::check m5-improper-mixed 2
:::

:::example From a mixed number to an improper fraction
Write $3\dfrac{2}{5}$ as an improper fraction.
@step Each whole is $\dfrac{5}{5}$, so $3$ wholes are $3 \times 5 = 15$ fifths.
@step Add the $2$ extra fifths: $15 + 2 = 17$.
@step Check by going back: $17 \div 5 = 3$ R $2$, which gives $3\dfrac{2}{5}$ again.
@answer $3\dfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{17}{5}$
:::

:::mistake Multiply, then add
Do not just add the whole number to the numerator. $3\dfrac{2}{5}$ is not $\dfrac{5}{5}$. Each whole is $5$ fifths, so multiply first: $3 \times 5 + 2 = 17$.
:::

## Fractions on a number line

On a number line, each whole is cut into equal spaces. On this line each whole has $4$ spaces, so each space is $\dfrac{1}{4}$.

:::figure
{"type":"numberLine","min":0,"max":3,"step":0.25,"points":[{"value":0.75,"label":"3/4"},{"value":1.75,"label":"7/4"},{"value":2.25,"label":"9/4"}]}
:::

$\dfrac{7}{4}$ is $7$ spaces from $0$. It is also $3$ spaces past $1$, so it is $1\dfrac{3}{4}$. An improper fraction and its mixed number are at the **same point** on the number line.

:::example Read a point
Where is $\dfrac{9}{4}$ on the number line above?
@step Each space is $\dfrac{1}{4}$. Count $9$ spaces from $0$.
@step $8$ spaces reach $2$. One more space goes past $2$.
@answer $\dfrac{9}{4}$ is $1$ space past $2$, at $2\dfrac{1}{4}$.
:::

:::mistake Count spaces, not tick marks
Between $0$ and $1$ there are $5$ tick marks but only $4$ spaces. The denominator is the number of **spaces** in one whole.
:::

## Benchmarks: 0, one half and 1

:::definition Benchmark
A **benchmark** is a friendly number that we compare other numbers to. For fractions we use $0$, $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $1$.
:::

- If the numerator is **half** of the denominator, the fraction is $\dfrac{1}{2}$, like $\dfrac{4}{8}$.
- If the numerator **equals** the denominator, the fraction is $1$, like $\dfrac{8}{8}$.
- If the numerator is **greater** than the denominator, the fraction is greater than $1$, like $\dfrac{11}{8}$.

:::figure
{"type":"numberLine","min":0,"max":2,"step":0.125,"points":[{"value":0.375,"label":"3/8"},{"value":0.5,"label":"1/2"},{"value":0.875,"label":"7/8"},{"value":1.375,"label":"11/8"}]}
:::

:::example Compare with one half
Is $\dfrac{5}{12}$ greater or less than $\dfrac{1}{2}$?
@step Double the numerator: $2 \times 5 = 10$.
@step $10$ is less than the denominator $12$. So $5$ is less than half of $12$.
@answer $\dfrac{5}{12}$ is less than $\dfrac{1}{2}$.
:::

:::mistake Look at both numbers
$\dfrac{5}{12}$ is not greater than $\dfrac{1}{2}$ just because $5 > 1$. Always compare the numerator with **its own** denominator.
:::

:::check m5-fraction-number-line 3
:::

:::summary
- An improper fraction, like $\dfrac{7}{4}$, has a numerator greater than its denominator. It is more than $1$.
- A mixed number, like $1\dfrac{3}{4}$, has a whole-number part and a fraction part.
- Improper to mixed: divide. The quotient is the wholes; the remainder is the pieces left over.
- Mixed to improper: multiply the wholes by the denominator, then add the extra pieces.
- A natural number can be written with denominator $1$: $3 = \dfrac{3}{1}$.
- An improper fraction and its mixed number are at the same point on a number line.
- Compare fractions to the benchmarks $0$, $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $1$ by looking at the numerator and the denominator.
:::
