$157$ students are going on a field trip, and each van holds $8$ students. The division gives $19$ R $5$, but you cannot order $19$ and a bit vans! In this lesson you will decide whether to multiply or divide, and what to do with a remainder.

## Multiply or divide?

Read the story and picture what is happening.

- **Multiply** when you put equal groups together and want the total.
- **Divide** when you share a total into equal groups, or want to know how many groups fit.

:::example Boxes of markers
A school orders $24$ boxes of markers. Each box holds $36$ markers. How many markers is that?
@step There are $24$ equal groups of $36$. Put them together: multiply.
@step $36 \times 24$: $36 \times 4 = 144$ and $36 \times 20 = 720$.
@step Add the partial products: $144 + 720 = 864$.
@step Estimate to check: $40 \times 20 = 800$. Close!
@answer The school orders $864$ markers.
:::

## What to do with a remainder

The same division can have different answers. It depends on the question.

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["Question","157 ÷ 8 = 19 R 5","Answer"],"rows":[["How many vans for 157 students?","5 students still need a ride","20 vans (round up)"],["How many cookies does each of 8 friends get?","5 cookies cannot be shared equally","19 cookies (drop the remainder)"],["How many cookies are left over?","The remainder is what is left","5 cookies (the remainder)"]]}
:::

:::example Vans for a field trip
$157$ students go on a field trip. Each van holds $8$ students. How many vans are needed?
@step Divide: $157 \div 8 = 19$ R $5$.
@step $19$ vans hold $152$ students. The other $5$ students still need a ride.
@answer Round up: $20$ vans are needed.
:::

:::example Sharing cookies
$157$ cookies are shared equally among $8$ friends. How many does each friend get? How many are left over?
@step Divide: $157 \div 8 = 19$ R $5$.
@step Each friend gets $19$ cookies. That uses $8 \times 19 = 152$ cookies.
@answer Each friend gets $19$ cookies, and $5$ are left over.
:::

:::mistake Always rounding the same way
Do not just write the quotient. Ask: "Does the remainder need its own group (round up)? Can it be left out (drop it)? Or is the remainder the answer?"
:::

## Two-step problems

Some problems need two steps. Find the first answer, then use it in the second step.

:::example Buses for skating
$6$ classes are going skating. Each class has $27$ students. Each bus holds $8$ students. How many buses are needed?
@step First find the number of students: $27 \times 6 = 162$.
@step Then divide by the bus size: $162 \div 8 = 20$ R $2$.
@step $20$ buses hold $160$ students. $2$ students still need a seat, so round up.
@answer $21$ buses are needed.
:::

:::tip Does it make sense?
After you solve a problem, read the question again. Check that your answer fits the story: a whole number of buses, enough seats for everyone, and the right unit.
:::

:::summary
- Multiply to put equal groups together. Divide to share or to find how many groups.
- A remainder can mean round up, drop the remainder, or answer with the remainder. The question decides.
- In a two-step problem, use the answer from the first step in the second step.
- Check your answer with an estimate and by reading the question again.
:::
