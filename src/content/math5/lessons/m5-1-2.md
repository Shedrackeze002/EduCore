At a track meet, the scoreboard shows times like $9.48$ and $9.5$ seconds. Who won? To answer, you compare decimals. Then a news story might round the crowd to "about $12\,000$ people". In this lesson you will compare, order and round numbers.

## Comparing numbers

We use three symbols to compare two numbers.

:::definition Comparison symbols
- $<$ means **is less than**: $4 < 9$.
- $>$ means **is greater than**: $9 > 4$.
- $=$ means **is equal to**: $0.5 = 0.50$.

The open side of $<$ or $>$ faces the greater number.
:::

To compare two numbers, line up the decimal points (or the ones digits). Then compare place by place from the **left**. The first place where the digits are different decides.

:::example Which is greater?
Compare $0.7$ and $0.65$. Use $<$, $>$ or $=$.
@step Add a zero so both numbers have two decimal places: $0.70$ and $0.65$.
@step The ones digits are both $0$. The tenths digits are $7$ and $6$.
@step $7 > 6$, so $0.70$ is greater.
@answer $0.7 > 0.65$
:::

:::mistake More digits does not mean bigger
$0.65$ has more digits than $0.7$, but it is smaller. Six tenths and five hundredths is less than seven tenths. Compare the places, not the number of digits.
:::

:::

## Decimals on a number line

A number line shows where each number sits. Numbers get greater as you move to the right.

:::figure
{"type":"numberLine","min":3.4,"max":3.5,"step":0.01,"points":[{"value":3.42,"label":"3.42"},{"value":3.47,"label":"3.47"}]}
:::

From $3.4$ to $3.5$ there are $10$ equal jumps. Each jump is one hundredth, $0.01$. So $3.47$ is $7$ jumps after $3.4$.

:::definition Between
A number is **between** two numbers when it is greater than one and less than the other. $3.47$ is between $3.4$ and $3.5$.
:::

There is always another decimal between two decimals. Add a zero to each one and look closer: between $2.4$ and $2.5$ are $2.41$, $2.42$, …, $2.49$. Between $2.41$ and $2.42$ are $2.411$, $2.412$, and so on. It never ends!

:::example A decimal in between
Find a decimal between $2.4$ and $2.5$.
@step Add a zero to each number: $2.40$ and $2.50$.
@step Any number from $2.41$ to $2.49$ is between them.
@answer $2.45$ is one answer. ($2.41$ or $2.48$ also work.)
:::

:::check m5-compare-order 2
:::

## Ordering numbers

To put numbers in order, give them all the same number of decimal places. Then compare them place by place.

:::example Order the jumps
Four long jumps measured $3.4$ m, $3.38$ m, $3.405$ m and $3.04$ m. Order them from least to greatest.
@step Give each number three decimal places: $3.400$, $3.380$, $3.405$, $3.040$.
@step The ones are all $3$. Compare the tenths: $3.040$ has $0$ tenths, so it is the least.
@step $3.380$ has $3$ tenths. $3.400$ and $3.405$ both have $4$ tenths, so compare the thousandths: $0 < 5$.
@answer $3.04$, $3.38$, $3.4$, $3.405$
:::

:::check m5-compare-order 3
:::

## Rounding

**Rounding** gives a number that is close and easier to use. The context tells you which place to round to. A swimming scoreboard might show tenths of a second. A road sign shows whole kilometres.

:::figure
{"type":"numberLine","min":3.4,"max":3.5,"step":0.01,"points":[{"value":3.47,"label":"3.47"}]}
:::

$3.47$ is between $3.4$ and $3.5$. It is closer to $3.5$.

How to round:

1. Find the digit in the rounding place.
2. Look at the digit just to its **right**.
3. If that digit is $5$ or more, round **up**. If it is $4$ or less, round **down**.

:::example Round to the nearest tenth
Round $3.47$ to the nearest tenth.
@step The tenths digit is $4$. The digit to its right is $7$.
@step $7$ is $5$ or more, so round up.
@answer $3.47$ rounds to $3.5$.
:::

:::example When a 9 rounds up
Round $2\,497\,856$ to the nearest ten thousand.
@step The ten thousands digit is $9$. The digit to its right is $7$, so round up.
@step $9$ ten thousands plus $1$ more is $10$ ten thousands, which is $1$ more hundred thousand.
@answer $2\,497\,856$ rounds to $2\,500\,000$.
:::

:::mistake Looking at the wrong digit
To round to the nearest **tenth**, look at the **hundredths** digit. Do not look at the last digit of the number.
:::

:::tip More decimal places, more precise
$2.4$ m, $2.38$ m and $2.375$ m could all describe the same table. A number with more decimal places is more **precise**. It tells the amount in smaller pieces.
:::

:::summary
- Compare numbers place by place from the left, after lining up the decimal points.
- Use $<$, $>$ and $=$. A zero at the right end of a decimal does not change its value.
- There is always another decimal between two decimals.
- To round, look at the digit just right of the rounding place: $5$ or more rounds up, $4$ or less rounds down.
- More decimal places means a more precise number.
:::
