A cook adds $\dfrac{1}{2}$ cup of milk and $\dfrac{3}{4}$ cup of water. A carpenter cuts $24\dfrac{3}{4}$ inches off a board. To find totals and leftovers like these, you need to add and subtract fractions and mixed numbers.

## Same denominators

When the denominators are the same, the pieces are the same size. Add or subtract the numerators. Keep the denominator.

:::example Paint for a door
Hana uses $\dfrac{3}{8}$ of a can of paint on a door and $\dfrac{2}{8}$ of the can on the trim. How much of the can does she use?
@step The denominators are the same, so add the numerators: $3 + 2 = 5$.
@answer $\dfrac{3}{8} + \dfrac{2}{8} = \dfrac{5}{8}$ of the can.
:::

:::example Subtracting tenths
Find $\dfrac{7}{10} - \dfrac{3}{10}$.
@step Subtract the numerators: $7 - 3 = 4$, so the answer is $\dfrac{4}{10}$.
@step Simplify: divide the top and bottom by $2$.
@answer $\dfrac{7}{10} - \dfrac{3}{10} = \dfrac{2}{5}$
:::

:::mistake Adding the denominators
$\dfrac{3}{8} + \dfrac{2}{8}$ is **not** $\dfrac{5}{16}$. The pieces are still eighths. Only the numerators are added.
:::

## Different denominators

You cannot add halves and quarters directly. The pieces are different sizes. First write both fractions with a common denominator.

:::definition Common denominator
A **common denominator** is a number that both denominators divide into. For halves and quarters, use $4$. For thirds and quarters, use $12$.
:::

The top strip shows that $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is the same as $\dfrac{2}{4}$. Now both amounts are in quarters, so they can be added.

:::example Milk and water
A recipe uses $\dfrac{1}{2}$ cup of milk and $\dfrac{3}{4}$ cup of water. How much liquid is that?
@step Common denominator $4$: $\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{2}{4}$.
@step Add: $\dfrac{2}{4} + \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{5}{4}$.
@step $\dfrac{5}{4}$ is $4$ quarters plus $1$ more quarter, so $\dfrac{5}{4} = 1\dfrac{1}{4}$.
@answer The recipe uses $1\dfrac{1}{4}$ cups of liquid.
:::

:::definition Improper fraction
An **improper fraction** has a numerator that is bigger than or equal to the denominator, like $\dfrac{5}{4}$. It is $1$ whole or more. You can write it as a mixed number: $\dfrac{5}{4} = 1\dfrac{1}{4}$.
:::

:::example Thirds and quarters
Find $\dfrac{2}{3} - \dfrac{1}{4}$.
@step Common denominator $12$: $\dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{8}{12}$ and $\dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{3}{12}$.
@step Subtract: $\dfrac{8}{12} - \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{5}{12}$.
@answer $\dfrac{2}{3} - \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{5}{12}$
:::

## Adding and subtracting mixed numbers

Work with the whole numbers and the fractions separately.

:::example Hours worked
Nathan works $3\dfrac{1}{2}$ hours on Saturday and $4\dfrac{3}{4}$ hours on Sunday. How many hours is that?
@step Common denominator $4$: $3\dfrac{1}{2} = 3\dfrac{2}{4}$.
@step Wholes: $3 + 4 = 7$. Fractions: $\dfrac{2}{4} + \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{5}{4} = 1\dfrac{1}{4}$.
@step $7 + 1\dfrac{1}{4} = 8\dfrac{1}{4}$
@answer Nathan works $8\dfrac{1}{4}$ hours.
:::

When you subtract, the first fraction may be too small. Then you **regroup**: take $1$ whole from the whole number and add it to the fraction.

:::example Ham at the deli
A deli has $5\dfrac{1}{4}$ kg of ham. It sells $2\dfrac{3}{8}$ kg. How much is left?
@step Common denominator $8$: $5\dfrac{1}{4} = 5\dfrac{2}{8}$.
@step You cannot take $\dfrac{3}{8}$ from $\dfrac{2}{8}$. Regroup $1$ whole as $\dfrac{8}{8}$: $5\dfrac{2}{8} = 4\dfrac{10}{8}$.
@step Wholes: $4 - 2 = 2$. Fractions: $\dfrac{10}{8} - \dfrac{3}{8} = \dfrac{7}{8}$.
@answer There are $2\dfrac{7}{8}$ kg of ham left.
:::

:::check m104-add-sub-fraction 3
:::

## Measuring with mixed numbers

Rulers and tape measures show inches cut into eighths or sixteenths. Lengths are written as mixed numbers, like $2\dfrac{3}{8}$ inches.

:::figure

:::

The bar on the ruler ends $3$ marks past the $2$. Each mark is $\dfrac{1}{8}$ inch, so the bar is $2\dfrac{3}{8}$ inches long.

:::example Trim for a window
Two pieces of trim are $24\dfrac{3}{8}$ in and $18\dfrac{5}{8}$ in long. What is the total length?
@step Wholes: $24 + 18 = 42$. Fractions: $\dfrac{3}{8} + \dfrac{5}{8} = \dfrac{8}{8} = 1$.
@answer $42 + 1 = 43$, so the trim is $43$ in long in total.
:::

:::example What is left of the board?
Diego cuts the two pieces in the cut list from a $96$ in board. Ignoring the saw cuts, how much of the board is left?
@step Add the pieces: $24\dfrac{3}{4} + 31\dfrac{5}{8} = 24\dfrac{6}{8} + 31\dfrac{5}{8} = 55\dfrac{11}{8} = 56\dfrac{3}{8}$ in.
@step Regroup the board: $96 = 95\dfrac{8}{8}$.
@step Subtract: $95\dfrac{8}{8} - 56\dfrac{3}{8} = 39\dfrac{5}{8}$.
@answer There is $39\dfrac{5}{8}$ in of the board left.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting to regroup the whole board
You cannot take $\dfrac{3}{8}$ from $96$ until you write $96$ as $95\dfrac{8}{8}$.
:::

:::tip The saw cut
A real saw blade turns about $\dfrac{1}{8}$ inch of wood into sawdust with each cut. On the job, leave a little extra.
:::

:::summary
- Same denominators: add or subtract the numerators and keep the denominator.
- Different denominators: rewrite with a common denominator first.
- An improper fraction like $\dfrac{5}{4}$ can be written as a mixed number, $1\dfrac{1}{4}$.
- Mixed numbers: work with the wholes and the fractions separately. Regroup $1$ whole when you need more to subtract from.
:::
