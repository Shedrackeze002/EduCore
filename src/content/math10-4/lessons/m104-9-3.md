Will it snow on your day off? Will you win the door prize at the staff party? **Probability** is the maths of chance. It helps you decide if something is worth the risk.

## How likely is it?

:::definition Outcome, event and probability
An **outcome** is one thing that can happen, like rolling a $4$. An **event** is the outcome or group of outcomes you want, like rolling an even number. **Probability** tells how likely an event is. It goes from $0$ (impossible) to $1$ (certain).
:::

:::figure

:::

At $0$, an event is impossible. At $1$, it is certain. Everything else is in between.

- **Impossible:** it can never happen. Rolling a $7$ on a regular die.
- **Unlikely:** it happens less than half the time.
- **Equally likely:** it happens exactly half the time. A coin landing heads.
- **Likely:** it happens more than half the time.
- **Certain:** it always happens. Rolling a number from $1$ to $6$.

:::example Picking from a bag
A bag has $9$ green marbles and $1$ red marble. Owen picks one without looking. How likely is it that he picks red?
@step Only $1$ of the $10$ marbles is red. That is much less than half.
@answer Picking red is **unlikely**.
:::

## Probability as a fraction

When every outcome has the same chance, use this rule:

:::definition Probability of an event
$$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$
A **favourable** outcome is one that fits the event.
:::

:::example Marbles in a bag
A bag has $3$ red, $5$ blue and $2$ green marbles. What is the probability of picking red?
@step Favourable outcomes: $3$ red marbles.
@step Total outcomes: $3 + 5 + 2 = 10$ marbles.
@answer $P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{3}{10}$
:::

:::example A spinner
The spinner above has $5$ equal sections numbered $1$ to $5$. What is the probability of spinning a number greater than $3$?
@step Favourable: $4$ and $5$, so $2$ outcomes.
@step Total: $5$ sections.
@answer $P(\text{greater than } 3) = \dfrac{2}{5}$
:::

:::mistake The wrong bottom number
The bottom of the fraction is **all** the outcomes. For the bag above, $P(\text{red})$ is $\dfrac{3}{10}$, not $\dfrac{3}{7}$. The $7$ only counts the marbles that are not red.
:::

## Decimals and percents

To change a probability to a decimal, divide the top by the bottom. To change the decimal to a percent, multiply by $100$.

:::example A deck of cards
A standard deck has $52$ cards in $4$ suits: hearts, diamonds, clubs and spades. Each suit has $13$ cards. What is the probability of picking a heart, as a decimal and as a percent?
@step $P(\text{heart}) = \dfrac{13}{52}$
@step Divide: $13 \div 52 = 0.25$
@step Multiply by $100$: $0.25 \times 100 = 25\%$
@answer The probability is $0.25$, or $25\%$.
:::

:::example A raffle
A raffle for the food bank sold $200$ tickets. Kiona bought $5$. What is her chance of winning, as a percent?
@step $P(\text{win}) = \dfrac{5}{200}$
@step $5 \div 200 = 0.025$, and $0.025 \times 100 = 2.5\%$
@answer Kiona has a $2.5\%$ chance of winning.
:::

## Two-step chance questions

:::example Not red
A bag has $4$ red, $6$ blue and $5$ yellow marbles. What is the probability of **not** picking red?
@step Total: $4 + 6 + 5 = 15$
@step Not red: $15 - 4 = 11$ marbles
@answer $P(\text{not red}) = \dfrac{11}{15}$
:::

:::example Which bag is better?
Bag A has $10$ marbles and $3$ are red. Bag B has $20$ marbles and $5$ are red. Which bag gives a better chance of red?
@step Bag A: $\dfrac{3}{10} = 30\%$
@step Bag B: $\dfrac{5}{20} = 25\%$
@answer Bag A gives the better chance, even though Bag B has more red marbles.
:::

:::mistake More is not always better
Bag B has more red marbles, but it also has more marbles in all. Compare the probabilities, not just the counts.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Probability goes from $0$ (impossible) to $1$ (certain). One half means equally likely.
- $P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$
- Divide to get a decimal. Multiply the decimal by $100$ to get a percent.
- $P(\text{not A})$: count the outcomes that are not A, then divide by the total.
- To compare chances, compare the probabilities, not the counts.
:::
