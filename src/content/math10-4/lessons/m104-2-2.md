"Use three quarters of the pail." "Two thirds of your shift is on the till." At work you often need a fraction of an amount. This lesson shows a simple two-step method that works every time.

## What "a fraction of" means

"$\dfrac{3}{4}$ of $20$ litres" means: cut the $20$ litres into $4$ equal parts, then take $3$ of those parts.

:::definition Fraction of an amount
To find a **fraction of** an amount:
1. **Divide** the amount by the denominator (the bottom number). This gives one part.
2. **Multiply** one part by the numerator (the top number).
:::

The strip stands for the whole pail of $20$ L. It is cut into $4$ equal parts, so each part is $5$ L. Three parts are shaded.

:::example Three quarters of a pail
Aiyana has a $20$ L pail of paint. She uses $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of it on a wall. How many litres does she use?
@step One part: $20 \div 4 = 5$, so $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of $20$ L is $5$ L.
@step Three parts: $3 \times 5 = 15$.
@answer Aiyana uses $15$ L of paint.
:::

:::mistake Dividing by the wrong number
Always divide by the **bottom** number first. For $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of $20$, do $20 \div 4$, not $20 \div 3$.
:::

## Fractions of time, mass and money

The same two steps work for hours, kilograms, dollars or people.

:::example Part of a shift
Kwame works a $9$-hour shift at a grocery store. He spends $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of the shift at the cash register. How many hours is that?
@step One part: $9 \div 3 = 3$ hours.
@step Two parts: $2 \times 3 = 6$ hours.
@answer Kwame spends $6$ hours at the cash register.
:::

The circle graph shows Kwame's shift. The cash register part is $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of the circle.

:::example Flour in a bakery
A bakery has a $64$ kg sack of flour. The bakers use $\dfrac{3}{8}$ of it today. How much flour do they use?
@step One part: $64 \div 8 = 8$ kg.
@step Three parts: $3 \times 8 = 24$ kg.
@answer They use $24$ kg of flour.
:::

:::tip Check that it makes sense
$\dfrac{3}{8}$ is less than half. Half of $64$ kg is $32$ kg, and $24$ kg is less than that. The answer makes sense.
:::

## What is left?

Sometimes two fractions are used, one after the other. Read carefully. Is each fraction **of the full amount**, or **of what is left**?

:::example Two fractions of the full tank
A farm truck has $36$ L of diesel. On Monday it uses $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of the full tank. On Tuesday it uses $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of the full tank. How much is left?
@step Monday: $36 \div 3 = 12$ L.
@step Tuesday: $36 \div 4 = 9$ L.
@step Left: $36 - 12 - 9 = 15$ L.
@answer There are $15$ L of diesel left.
:::

:::example A fraction of what is left
Talia is paid \$480. She spends $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of it on rent. Then she spends $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of **what is left** on groceries. How much money does she have left?
@step Rent: $480 \div 4 = 120$. Left: $480 - 120 = 360$.
@step Groceries: $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of $360$ is $360 \div 3 = 120$. Left: $360 - 120 = 240$.
@step Check: $120 + 120 + 240 = 480$.
@answer Talia has \$240 left.
:::

:::mistake Using the wrong amount
In the second example, the groceries are $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of **\$360**, not $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of \$480. The words "of what is left" tell you to use the new amount.
:::

:::summary
- To find a fraction of an amount, divide by the denominator, then multiply by the numerator.
- $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of an amount is one of $4$ equal parts.
- Check: a fraction less than $1$ always gives less than the whole amount.
- For two steps, decide if each fraction is of the full amount or of what is left.
:::
