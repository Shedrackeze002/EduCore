If you invite two friends to a concert, inviting Ava and Kwame is the same as inviting Kwame and Ava. When the order of the choice does not matter, you are counting combinations, and there are fewer of them than permutations.

## Does order matter?

Before you count, decide whether a different order gives a different result.

| Situation | Different order, different result? | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Electing a president and a treasurer | yes: the jobs are different | permutation |
| Choosing 3 toppings for a pizza | no: same pizza | combination |
| Awarding gold, silver and bronze | yes: the medals are different | permutation |
| Choosing 5 players for a tournament | no: same group | combination |
| Setting a 4-digit PIN | yes: 1234 is not 4321 | permutation |
| Being dealt a hand of cards | no: same hand | combination |

:::tip The swap test
Swap two of the items you chose. If the result changes (a different job, place or position), order matters and it is a permutation. If not, it is a combination.
:::

## Counting combinations

Choosing 2 of 4 friends (A, B, C, D) in order gives ${}_{4}P_{2} = 12$ arrangements, but each pair appears twice (AB and BA). So there are $12 \div 2 = 6$ pairs. In general, every group of $r$ objects can be ordered in $r!$ ways, so divide the permutations by $r!$.

:::definition Combination
A **combination** is a selection in which order does not matter. The number of combinations of $n$ different objects taken $r$ at a time is
$${}_{n}C_{r} = \frac{{}_{n}P_{r}}{r!} = \frac{n!}{(n - r)!\,r!}$$
:::

:::example Inviting friends
Jessie has 3 extra tickets to give to 3 of 8 friends. How many different groups can be invited?
@step The friends all get the same kind of ticket, so order does not matter.
@step ${}_{8}C_{3} = \dfrac{8!}{5!\,3!} = \dfrac{8 \times 7 \times 6}{3 \times 2 \times 1}$
@answer $56$ groups.
:::

## Choosing from more than one group

When a selection needs some items from one group **and** some from another, count each choice with a combination and multiply.

:::example A talent show act
An act needs 2 of 6 singers and 3 of 7 dancers. How many selections are possible?
@step Singers: ${}_{6}C_{2} = 15$. Dancers: ${}_{7}C_{3} = 35$.
@answer $15 \times 35 = 525$ selections.
:::

A standard deck of 52 cards has four suits: hearts and diamonds (red), clubs and spades (black). Each suit has 13 ranks: A, 2 to 10, J, Q and K.

:::example A pair
How many 2-card hands are a pair (two cards of the same rank)?
@step Choose the rank: 13 ways.
@step Choose 2 of the 4 cards of that rank: ${}_{4}C_{2} = 6$.
@answer $13 \times 6 = 78$ pairs.
:::

## Cases and "at least"

:::example Toppings
A sandwich shop offers 6 toppings. A customer must choose at least one topping. How many topping combinations are possible?
@step Each topping is either on or off: $2^6 = 64$ patterns.
@step One pattern has no toppings, which is not allowed.
@answer $64 - 1 = 63$ combinations. (This is ${}_{6}C_{1} + {}_{6}C_{2} + \cdots + {}_{6}C_{6}$.)
:::

:::example At least one teen
A food bank needs 4 volunteers. Five adults and four teens have signed up. How many groups include at least one teen?
@step All groups: ${}_{9}C_{4} = 126$.
@step Groups with no teens (adults only): ${}_{5}C_{4} = 5$.
@answer $126 - 5 = 121$ groups.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting that choosing and arranging is a permutation
Choosing 3 of 8 paintings is ${}_{8}C_{3} = 56$. Hanging those 3 in a row multiplies by $3! = 6$, giving $336 = {}_{8}P_{3}$. If the question cares about the order, a combination alone is not enough.
:::

:::check m302-combinations 3
:::

:::summary
- Use the swap test: if order matters it is a permutation; if not, a combination.
- ${}_{n}C_{r} = \dfrac{n!}{(n - r)!\,r!}$, which is ${}_{n}P_{r}$ divided by $r!$.
- For choices from several groups, multiply the combinations.
- For "at least one", subtract the selections with none from the total.
- Choosing and then arranging: ${}_{n}C_{r} \times r! = {}_{n}P_{r}$.
:::
