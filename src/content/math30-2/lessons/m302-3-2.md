What is the chance that a randomly chosen committee is all Grade 12 students, or that two friends end up sitting side by side? When there are too many outcomes to list, the counting methods from Unit 2 count them for you.

## Counting favourable and total outcomes

When outcomes are equally likely,
$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$
Count both the numerator and the denominator with the fundamental counting principle, permutations or combinations.

:::example Lining up for a photo
Five friends, including Emeka and Bao, line up in a random order. What is the probability that Emeka is first and Bao is second?
@step All orders: $5! = 120$
@step Favourable: Emeka and Bao are placed, and the other 3 friends can stand in any order: $3! = 6$
@answer $P = \frac{6}{120} = \frac{1}{20}$
:::

:::tip Order matters or not?
Use permutations (${}_{n}P_{r}$, $n!$) when the order or position matters, and combinations (${}_{n}C_{r}$) when only the group matters. Use the same method for the favourable outcomes and the total.
:::

## Probability with combinations

:::example A random committee
Four Grade 12 students and six Grade 11 students volunteer. A committee of 3 is chosen at random. What is the probability that all 3 are in Grade 12? That exactly 2 are?
@step All committees: ${}_{10}C_{3} = 120$
@step All Grade 12: ${}_{4}C_{3} = 4$, so $P = \frac{4}{120} = \frac{1}{30}$.
@step Exactly 2 in Grade 12: choose 2 of the 4 **and** 1 of the 6: ${}_{4}C_{2} \times {}_{6}C_{1} = 6 \times 6 = 36$
@answer $P(\text{all Grade 12}) = \frac{1}{30}$ and $P(\text{exactly 2}) = \frac{36}{120} = \frac{3}{10}$
:::

:::example Two hearts
Two cards are dealt from a standard deck of 52 (13 of each suit). What is the probability that both are hearts?
@step All 2-card hands: ${}_{52}C_{2} = 1326$
@step Hands with two hearts: ${}_{13}C_{2} = 78$
@answer $P = \frac{78}{1326} = \frac{1}{17}$
:::

:::mistake Mixing methods
If you count the favourable outcomes with combinations, count the total with combinations too. Using ${}_{52}P_{2}$ on the bottom and ${}_{13}C_{2}$ on the top gives an answer that is half the correct one.
:::

## Restrictions and "at least one"

:::example Sitting together
Six students, including Ava and Ben, sit in a row in a random order. What is the probability that Ava and Ben sit together?
@step All arrangements: $6! = 720$
@step Treat Ava and Ben as one block. The block and the other 4 students make 5 items: $5!$ arrangements. Inside the block, Ava and Ben can switch: $2!$.
@step Favourable: $5! \times 2! = 120 \times 2 = 240$
@answer $P = \frac{240}{720} = \frac{1}{3}$
:::

"At least one" means one or more. It is usually easier to find the probability of **none** and subtract from 1.

:::example At least one Grade 12 student
For the committee of 3 chosen from 4 Grade 12 and 6 Grade 11 students, what is the probability that at least one member is in Grade 12?
@step No Grade 12 students: all 3 from the 6 Grade 11 students: ${}_{6}C_{3} = 20$, so $P(\text{none}) = \frac{20}{120} = \frac{1}{6}$.
@answer $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{5}{6}$
:::

:::example Repeated letters
The letters of CANADA are arranged at random. What is the probability that the arrangement begins and ends with A?
@step CANADA has 6 letters with three A's: $\frac{6!}{3!} = 120$ arrangements.
@step Put an A at each end. The middle letters C, N, A, D are all different: $4! = 24$ arrangements.
@answer $P = \frac{24}{120} = \frac{1}{5}$
:::

:::check m302-prob-counting 3
:::

:::summary
- $P = \dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{total}}$; count both with the same method.
- Use permutations when order matters and combinations when it does not.
- For choices from two groups, multiply the combinations for each group.
- Treat items that must be together as one block, then arrange inside the block.
- $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$.
:::
