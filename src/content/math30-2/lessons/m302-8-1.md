Puzzles and games have been part of mathematics for thousands of years. Solving them uses the same habits as any hard problem: organize the information, look for patterns, test ideas, and check your answer against every condition.

## Strategies for solving puzzles

:::definition Strategy
A **strategy** is a general plan for solving a problem or winning a game. Useful strategies include:
- making an organized list or table,
- guessing, checking and improving the guess,
- eliminating possibilities,
- looking for a pattern, and
- working backward from the goal.
:::

:::example A number riddle
I am a two-digit number. The sum of my digits is 11, and my tens digit is 3 more than my ones digit. What number am I?
@step Make an organized list of two-digit numbers with a digit sum of 11: 29, 38, 47, 56, 65, 74, 83, 92.
@step Check the second clue for each one: only 74 has a tens digit 3 more than its ones digit ($7 = 4 + 3$).
@answer The number is 74.
:::

:::tip Check every clue
When you think you have the answer, test it against every clue. A good puzzle has exactly one answer that fits them all.
:::

## Magic squares and Sudoku

In a **magic square**, every row, every column and both diagonals add to the same number, the magic sum. In a 4 by 4 **Sudoku**, each row, each column and each 2 by 2 box contains the digits 1 to 4 exactly once.

:::example A 4 by 4 Sudoku
What digit goes in cell B1 (column B, row 1)?
@step Row 1 already has 1 and 4, so B1 is 2 or 3.
@step Column B already has 4 and 3, so B1 cannot be 3.
@answer B1 $= 2$
:::

:::mistake Guessing too early
If a cell still has two possible digits, do not guess. Fill in cells that have only one possibility first; each one you fill gives you more information.
:::

## Logic puzzles

In a logic puzzle, clues match people with choices. A grid with ✓ for "yes" and ✗ for "no" keeps track of what you know.

:::example Who plays curling?
Ava, Bao and Connor each play a different sport: hockey, soccer or curling. Ava does not play hockey. The person who plays curling is neither Ava nor Bao. Who plays each sport?
@step The curling player is not Ava or Bao, so Connor plays curling.
@step Ava does not play hockey or curling, so Ava plays soccer.
@answer Ava plays soccer, Bao plays hockey and Connor plays curling.
:::

## Games and winning strategies

Many games have a **winning strategy**: a way to play that guarantees a win, whatever the other player does. To find one, work backward from the end of the game.

:::example A take-away game
Two players take turns removing 1, 2 or 3 counters from a pile of 15. The player who takes the last counter wins. How many should the first player take?
@step If you leave your opponent 4 counters, whatever they take (1, 2 or 3), you can take the rest. So 4 is a losing position for the player about to move.
@step In the same way, 8 and 12 are losing positions: if your opponent takes $t$, you take $4 - t$, and the pile drops by 4 each round.
@step From 15, taking 3 leaves 12, a multiple of 4.
@answer Take 3 counters, then always make each round total 4.
:::

Some puzzles need a systematic search instead. With an empty 3 L jug, an empty 5 L jug and a tap, you can measure exactly 4 L in 6 steps: fill the 5 L jug, pour it into the 3 L jug (leaving 2 L), empty the 3 L jug, pour the 2 L into it, fill the 5 L jug again, and top up the 3 L jug, which leaves 4 L in the 5 L jug.

:::

:::summary
- Useful strategies: organized lists, guess and check, eliminating possibilities, looking for patterns and working backward.
- Magic squares: find the magic sum from a complete line, then fill lines with one blank.
- Sudoku and logic puzzles: fill in what is certain first, and use each new fact to rule out others.
- In take-away games, work backward to find the losing positions, and always leave your opponent in one.
:::
