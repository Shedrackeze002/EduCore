Six signal flags hung on a pole can send $720$ different messages, but only if every flag is a different colour. If three of the flags are red, many of those "messages" look exactly the same. This lesson deals with identical objects and with rules about where things can go.

## Identical objects

Arrange the letters A, A and B. If the two As were different ($\text{A}_1$ and $\text{A}_2$), there would be $3! = 6$ arrangements. But you cannot tell $\text{A}_1\text{A}_2\text{B}$ from $\text{A}_2\text{A}_1\text{B}$, so each arrangement is counted $2! = 2$ times. There are only $\dfrac{3!}{2!} = 3$: AAB, ABA and BAA.

:::definition Arrangements with identical objects
The number of arrangements of $n$ objects, where $a$ are identical, $b$ are identical, and so on, is
$$\frac{n!}{a!\,b!\cdots}$$
:::

:::example Signal flags
Three red, two blue and one white flag are hung one above another on a pole. Flags of the same colour look identical. How many different signals can be made using all six flags?
@step $n = 6$, with 3 alike (red) and 2 alike (blue).
@step $\dfrac{6!}{3!\,2!} = \dfrac{720}{6 \times 2}$
@answer $60$ signals.
:::

:::mistake Using n! when objects are identical
$6! = 720$ treats the three red flags as if they were different. Swapping two red flags does not change the signal, so you must divide.
:::

## Fixed positions

When a rule fixes where something goes, deal with that rule first. Then arrange everything else.

:::example The captain in the middle
Five friends line up for a team photo, and the captain must stand in the middle. How many line-ups are possible?
@step The captain has 1 spot.
@step The other 4 friends fill the other 4 spots: $4! = 24$.
@answer $1 \times 24 = 24$ line-ups.
:::

:::example At one end
Six friends line up, and Kai must stand at one of the two ends. How many line-ups are possible?
@step Kai has 2 choices of spot.
@step The other 5 friends fill the remaining spots: $5! = 120$.
@answer $2 \times 120 = 240$ line-ups.
:::

## Together or apart

- **Must be together:** treat the pair as one unit. Arrange the units, then multiply by the ways to order the pair inside its unit.
- **Must be apart:** count all arrangements, then subtract the ones where they are together.

:::example Twins in a line
Six students, including a pair of twins, line up. How many line-ups have the twins (a) together and (b) apart?
@step (a) The twins form one unit, so there are 5 units: $5! = 120$. The twins can swap: $2 \times 120 = 240$.
@step (b) All line-ups: $6! = 720$. Apart: $720 - 240 = 480$.
@answer (a) $240$; (b) $480$
:::

When the objects in the block are identical, the block can only be arranged one way inside.

:::example Trees side by side
Three pine, two spruce and two aspen trees are planted in a row. Trees of the same type look identical. The aspens must be side by side. How many planting orders are possible?
@step Treat the two aspens as one block: units are 3 pine, 2 spruce and 1 block, so 6 units.
@step $\dfrac{6!}{3!\,2!} = \dfrac{720}{12} = 60$. The identical aspens inside the block add no new orders.
@answer $60$ planting orders.
:::

## Alternating patterns

:::example Boys and girls alternate
Three boys and three girls sit in a row of six chairs for a choir photo, alternating boy and girl. How many seatings are possible?
@step Two patterns: B G B G B G or G B G B G B.
@step In each pattern, arrange the boys ($3! = 6$) and the girls ($3! = 6$).
@answer $2 \times 6 \times 6 = 72$ seatings.
:::

:::tip One more of one group
With 4 boys and 3 girls, the row must start and end with a boy, so there is only one pattern: $4! \times 3! = 144$ seatings.
:::

:::check m302-permutations-special 3
:::

:::summary
- With identical objects, divide $n!$ by the factorial of each group of identical objects.
- Place restricted objects first, then arrange the rest.
- "Together": treat the group as one unit and multiply by the arrangements inside it.
- "Apart": total arrangements minus "together" arrangements.
- For alternating patterns, count the possible patterns, then arrange each group in its spots.
:::
