A straight line, a parabola and an S-shaped curve can all be described by one family of equations: polynomial functions. A few numbers in the equation tell you a lot about the shape of the graph before you draw it.

## Polynomial functions and degree

:::definition Polynomial function
A function whose terms are whole-number powers of $x$ with real coefficients, such as $y = 4 + 3x - 2x^3$. The **degree** is the highest power of $x$. The **leading coefficient** is the coefficient of that term, and the **constant term** is the term with no $x$.
:::

In this course the degree is at most 3.

| Degree | Name | Standard form | Shape of graph |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | linear | $y = ax + b$ | straight line |
| 2 | quadratic | $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ | parabola |
| 3 | cubic | $y = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d$ | S-shaped curve |

## End behaviour

:::definition End behaviour
The direction the graph goes at the far left and the far right. We describe it by the quadrants where the graph starts and ends.
:::

Only the leading term matters: the degree decides whether the two ends go the same way, and the sign of the leading coefficient decides which way the right end goes.

| | Leading coefficient positive | Leading coefficient negative |
|---|---|---|
| Odd degree (1 or 3) | quadrant III to quadrant I (down, then up) | quadrant II to quadrant IV (up, then down) |
| Even degree (2) | quadrant II to quadrant I (both ends up) | quadrant III to quadrant IV (both ends down) |

:::example End behaviour from the equation
Describe the end behaviour of $y = 4 + 3x - 2x^3$.
@step The degree, $3$, is odd, so the ends go in opposite directions.
@step The leading coefficient, $-2$, is negative, so the right end goes down.
@answer The graph extends from quadrant II to quadrant IV.
:::

## Intercepts and turning points

The **$y$-intercept** is the value when $x = 0$: in standard form it is the constant term. The **$x$-intercepts** are the zeros of the function. In factored form you can read them straight from the factors.

:::definition Turning point
A point where the graph changes from increasing to decreasing, or from decreasing to increasing.
:::

A polynomial function of degree $n$ has **at most $n$ $x$-intercepts** and **at most $n - 1$ turning points**.

A cubic must cross the $x$-axis at least once, because its ends go in opposite directions, so it has 1, 2 or 3 $x$-intercepts. It has either 2 turning points or none (like $y = x^3$). A parabola has 0, 1 or 2 $x$-intercepts and exactly 1 turning point.

## Domain and range

Any real number can be substituted into a polynomial, so the **domain is always** $\{x \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$. The range depends on the end behaviour.

- Linear (not horizontal) and cubic functions: one end goes down forever and the other goes up forever, so the range is $\{y \mid y \in \mathbb{R}\}$.
- Quadratic functions: the vertex is a maximum or a minimum, so the range stops there.

:::example A quadratic and a cubic
State the domain and range of $y = -(x - 1)^2 + 4$ and of $y = x^3 - 4x + 1$.
@step Quadratic: the vertex is $(1, 4)$ and $a = -1 < 0$, so the parabola opens down and $4$ is the maximum value.
@step Cubic: odd degree, so the graph extends from quadrant III to quadrant I and takes every $y$-value.
@answer Both domains are $\{x \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$. Ranges: $\{y \mid y \le 4,\ y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ for the quadratic and $\{y \mid y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ for the cubic.
:::

To match an equation with a graph, combine the clues: end behaviour from the leading term, the $y$-intercept from the constant term, and the possible number of $x$-intercepts and turning points from the degree.

:::check m302-poly-features 3
:::

:::summary
- The degree is the highest power of $x$; the leading coefficient multiplies that term; the constant term is the $y$-intercept.
- Odd degree: ends go in opposite directions. Even degree: both ends go the same way. A positive leading coefficient sends the right end up.
- Degree $n$: at most $n$ $x$-intercepts and at most $n - 1$ turning points; a squared factor makes the graph touch the $x$-axis.
- The domain is all real numbers; linear and cubic ranges are all real numbers, and a quadratic's range stops at its vertex.
:::
