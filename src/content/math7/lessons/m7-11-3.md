If you flip two coins, how often should you get two heads? You can work it out with a sample space, or you can flip the coins and count. In this lesson you will compare these two kinds of probability.

## Theoretical probability of two events

:::definition Theoretical probability
The **theoretical probability** of an event is what *should* happen, found from the sample space when every outcome is equally likely:
$$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes in the sample space}}$$
:::

:::

:::example Heads and an even number
Noor flips a coin and rolls a six-sided die. What is the probability of heads and an even number?
@step The table shows $2 \times 6 = 12$ equally likely outcomes.
@step Favourable outcomes: H2, H4, H6. That is $3$ outcomes.
@step $P(\text{heads and even}) = \dfrac{3}{12}$
@answer $P(\text{heads and even}) = \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{1}{4}$, or $25\%$
:::

## Sums of two dice and fair games

When you roll two dice, there are $6 \times 6 = 36$ outcomes. A table of sums makes the favourable outcomes easy to count.

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["Red ↓ Blue →","1","2","3","4","5","6"],"rows":[["1","2","3","4","5","6","7"],["2","3","4","5","6","7","8"],["3","4","5","6","7","8","9"],["4","5","6","7","8","9","10"],["5","6","7","8","9","10","11"],["6","7","8","9","10","11","12"]]}
:::

:::example A sum of 7
Lucas rolls two dice. What is the probability that the sum is $7$?
@step Count the $7$s in the table: $(1, 6)$, $(2, 5)$, $(3, 4)$, $(4, 3)$, $(5, 2)$, $(6, 1)$. That is $6$ outcomes.
@step $P(\text{sum of } 7) = \dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac{1}{6}$
@step As a percent: $1 \div 6 \approx 0.1667$, so about $17\%$
@answer $P(\text{sum of } 7) = \dfrac{1}{6}$, or about $17\%$
:::

:::definition Fair game
A game is **fair** when every player has the same probability of winning.
:::

:::example Is the game fair?
Mei and Owen roll two dice. Mei scores if the sum is even. Owen scores if the sum is odd. Is the game fair?
@step Count the even sums in the table: $18$ of the $36$ cells.
@step $P(\text{Mei}) = \dfrac{18}{36} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $P(\text{Owen}) = \dfrac{18}{36} = \dfrac{1}{2}$
@answer Yes. Both players have a probability of $\dfrac{1}{2}$, so the game is fair.
:::

## Experimental probability

:::definition Experimental probability
The **experimental probability** of an event is what *did* happen when you did the experiment:
$$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{number of times the event happened}}{\text{total number of trials}}$$
A **trial** is one time you do the experiment.
:::

:::figure
{"type":"table","headers":["Outcome","Frequency"],"rows":[["HH","12"],["HT","9"],["TH","11"],["TT","8"]]}
:::

:::example Flipping two coins
Kiona flips a loonie and a quarter $40$ times. The table shows her results. Find the experimental and theoretical probabilities of HH.
@step Experimental: HH happened $12$ times in $40$ trials, so $P = \dfrac{12}{40} = \dfrac{3}{10} = 30\%$.
@step Theoretical: the sample space is HH, HT, TH, TT, so $P(\text{HH}) = \dfrac{1}{4} = 25\%$.
@answer The experimental probability ($30\%$) is greater than the theoretical probability ($25\%$).
:::

## Why the results are different

Chance means results jump around. In a small experiment, the experimental probability can be far from the theoretical probability. As the number of trials grows, the experimental probability usually gets **closer** to the theoretical probability.

:::example More trials
Kiona’s class wants to know the probability of HH. Kiona did $20$ trials and got HH $8$ times. The whole class did $400$ trials and got HH $103$ times.
@step Kiona: $\dfrac{8}{20} = 40\%$
@step The class: $\dfrac{103}{400} = 25.75\%$
@step The theoretical probability is $25\%$.
@answer The class result is much closer to $25\%$, because it uses many more trials.
:::

You can also use the theoretical probability to predict results:

$$\text{expected number} = \text{theoretical probability} \times \text{number of trials}$$

If you roll two dice $120$ times, expect a sum of $7$ about $\dfrac{1}{6} \times 120 = 20$ times. Getting $17$ or $23$ is normal.

:::mistake “The experiment went wrong”
An experimental probability that does not match the theoretical probability does not mean the coins are unfair or the maths is wrong. Small differences are normal. Do more trials before you decide.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Theoretical probability: favourable outcomes ÷ outcomes in the sample space.
- Experimental probability: number of times it happened ÷ number of trials.
- A game is fair when every player has the same probability of winning.
- Experimental results vary. With more trials, they usually get closer to the theoretical probability.
- Expected number $=$ theoretical probability $\times$ number of trials.
:::
