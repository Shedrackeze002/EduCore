Look around your classroom. The lines on a sheet of paper never meet. The edges of a door meet in a square corner. These two kinds of line segments, parallel and perpendicular, are everywhere: on hockey rinks, in buildings and on maps.

## Parallel and perpendicular

:::definition Line segment
A **line segment** is the part of a line between two endpoints. Segment $AB$ has endpoints $A$ and $B$.
:::

:::definition Parallel line segments
**Parallel** line segments are always the same distance apart. They never meet, even if you make them longer. We write $AB \parallel CD$.
:::

:::definition Perpendicular line segments
**Perpendicular** line segments meet at a right angle, $90^\circ$. A small square in the corner marks the right angle. We write $EF \perp GH$.
:::

:::example Lines on a hockey rink
On a hockey rink, are these pairs parallel or perpendicular? (a) the two blue lines (b) a blue line and the side boards
@step The two blue lines go straight across the rink. They stay the same distance apart and never meet.
@step A blue line meets the side boards in a square corner.
@answer (a) The two blue lines are parallel. (b) A blue line and the side boards are perpendicular.
:::

:::mistake Not touching is not enough
Two segments that do not touch are not always parallel. If they get closer together, they would meet if you made them longer. Parallel segments stay the same distance apart **everywhere**.
:::

## Checking sides on a grid

On grid paper, count squares to check.

- Two sides are **parallel** when they make the same move: the same number of squares across and the same number up or down. Two flat (horizontal) sides are parallel too.
- A flat (horizontal) side and a straight up-and-down (vertical) side are **perpendicular**.

:::example The sides of a trapezoid
Name the parallel sides and the perpendicular sides of quadrilateral $ABCD$.
@step $AB$ is flat. $DC$ is flat too, so $AB \parallel DC$.
@step $AD$ goes straight up. It meets $AB$ and $DC$ in square corners, so $AD \perp AB$ and $AD \perp DC$.
@step $BC$ goes $2$ squares left and $4$ up. No other side makes that move, and it makes no square corner.
@answer $AB \parallel DC$, $AD \perp AB$ and $AD \perp DC$. Side $BC$ is neither.
:::

:::example Slanted sides
Which sides of quadrilateral $PQRS$ are parallel?
@step From $P$ to $Q$: $4$ right and $1$ down. From $S$ to $R$: $4$ right and $1$ down. Same move, so $PQ \parallel SR$.
@step From $P$ to $S$: $2$ right and $3$ up. From $Q$ to $R$: $2$ right and $3$ up. So $PS \parallel QR$.
@answer $PQ \parallel SR$ and $PS \parallel QR$. A quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides is a parallelogram.
:::

:::example A missing angle
$AB \perp CD$ at point $E$. Ray $EF$ makes a $35^\circ$ angle with $EA$. Find $\angle FEC$, marked $x$.
@step $AB$ and $CD$ are perpendicular, so $\angle AEC = 90^\circ$.
@step $\angle AEF + \angle FEC = 90^\circ$
@step $x = 90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$
@answer $\angle FEC = 55^\circ$.
:::

Parallel segments are the same distance apart everywhere. To check, measure the distance between them at both ends, at right angles to one segment.

:::example Is it parallel?
Mason draws segment $CD$ below segment $AB$. The distance between them is $3.2$ cm at the left end and $3.6$ cm at the right end. Are they parallel?
@step The distances are different: $3.2$ cm and $3.6$ cm.
@step The segments get farther apart to the right, so they would meet if you made them longer to the left.
@answer No, $AB$ and $CD$ are not parallel.
:::

:::mistake Length does not matter
Parallel segments do not have to be the same length. Only the distance between them matters.
:::

## Constructing a perpendicular segment

You can construct a perpendicular segment through point $P$ on segment $AB$ with a compass and a ruler.

1. Place the compass point on $P$. Draw an arc on each side of $P$ that crosses $AB$. Label the crossings $C$ and $D$.
2. Open the compass wider. Place the compass point on $C$ and draw an arc above $AB$.
3. Keep the same opening. Place the compass point on $D$ and draw an arc that crosses the last arc. Label the crossing $E$.
4. Use the ruler to join $P$ and $E$. Segment $PE$ is perpendicular to $AB$.

## Constructing a parallel segment

**With a set square** (a plastic right triangle) and a ruler:

1. Line up one edge of the set square exactly along $AB$.
2. Place the ruler firmly against another edge of the set square.
3. Hold the ruler still. Slide the set square along it until its first edge passes through $P$.
4. Draw a segment through $P$ along that edge. It is parallel to $AB$.

**With two perpendiculars:** construct two segments perpendicular to $AB$, each the same length as the distance from $AB$ to $P$. Join their ends. The new segment is the same distance from $AB$ everywhere, so it is parallel to $AB$.

:::summary
- Parallel segments ($\parallel$) are always the same distance apart and never meet.
- Perpendicular segments ($\perp$) meet at a right angle, $90^\circ$.
- On a grid, parallel sides make the same move. Horizontal and vertical sides are perpendicular.
- Use $90^\circ$ to find missing angles. Measure the distance at both ends to check for parallel segments.
- Construct a perpendicular with compass arcs, and a parallel with a set square or with two perpendiculars.
:::
