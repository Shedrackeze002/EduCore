What happens to $f(x)$ as $x$ gets closer and closer to a number $a$ — even when $f(a)$ itself is undefined, or is something unexpected? That question, the **limit**, is the foundation of all of calculus.

## The idea of a limit

:::definition Limit
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means that $f(x)$ can be made as close to $L$ as we like by taking $x$ close enough to $a$, on either side, **but not equal to** $a$.
:::

The last three words matter. A limit describes where the graph is heading as $x$ approaches $a$; it says nothing about what happens exactly at $x = a$.

:::mistake The limit is not the function value
Reading the closed dot gives $f(a)$, not the limit. The two can be different, and the limit can exist even when $f(a)$ is undefined.
:::

## One-sided limits

Sometimes the graph approaches different heights from the two sides. Then we look at each side separately.

:::definition One-sided limits
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ (the **left-hand limit**) uses only $x < a$; $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ (the **right-hand limit**) uses only $x > a$.
:::

:::example A jump
Use the graph above to find the one-sided limits at $x = 2$.
@step From the left, the line rises toward the closed dot at $(2, 1)$: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = 1$.
@step From the right, the curve heads toward the open circle at $(2, 4)$: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 4$.
@step The closed dot shows $f(2) = 1$.
@answer $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = 1$, $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 4$ and $f(2) = 1$
:::

The two-sided limit and the one-sided limits are linked by one rule:

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \quad \text{exactly when} \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \ \text{and} \ \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L.$$

In the example, the one-sided limits are $1$ and $4$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ does not exist.

:::tip An open circle still counts
A one-sided limit can equal the height of an open circle. The graph never reaches that point, but it gets as close as you like to it — that is all a limit needs.
:::

## When a limit does not exist

A limit fails to exist for one of three reasons:

- **A jump:** the left-hand and right-hand limits are different, as at $x = 2$ above.
- **Unbounded values:** $f(x)$ grows without bound, as $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ does near $x = 0$. (Section 2.3 writes this as $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x^2} = \infty$.)
- **Oscillation:** $f(x)$ keeps swinging between values, as $\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ does near $x = 0$.

:::example A table that splits
For $f(x) = \dfrac{|x|}{x}$, find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ if it exists.
@step For $x < 0$, $|x| = -x$, so $f(x) = -1$: the left-hand limit is $-1$.
@step For $x > 0$, $|x| = x$, so $f(x) = 1$: the right-hand limit is $1$.
@answer The one-sided limits differ, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{|x|}{x}$ does not exist.
:::

## Estimating limits from tables

When you cannot see a graph, compute $f(x)$ for values of $x$ closer and closer to $a$ from both sides, and look for the number the outputs close in on.

:::example The limit of sin x over x
Estimate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$, with $x$ in radians.
@step Substituting $x = 0$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$, which tells us nothing, so make a table.
@step From the left and from the right the values are $0.9983342$, $0.9999833$, $0.9999998$.
@answer The values approach $1$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$.
:::

:::summary
- $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means $f(x)$ approaches $L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from both sides; the value $f(a)$ does not matter.
- Read one-sided limits from the piece of the graph on each side, including open circles.
- The two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.
- A limit fails to exist at a jump, where the values are unbounded, or where they oscillate.
- In a table, use $x$-values approaching $a$ from both sides and look for the common value.
:::
