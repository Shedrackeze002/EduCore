Informally, a function is continuous if you can draw its graph without lifting your pencil. Calculus needs a precise version of that idea, and limits give us one.

## The definition of continuity

:::definition Continuous at a point
A function $f$ is **continuous** at $x = a$ when all three conditions hold:
1. $f(a)$ is defined;
2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists;
3. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.

If any condition fails, $f$ has a **discontinuity** at $x = a$.
:::

Check the conditions in order: the first one that fails tells you the type of discontinuity.

:::definition Types of discontinuity
- **Removable** (a hole): the limit exists, but $f(a)$ is undefined or different from it.
- **Jump:** the one-sided limits exist but are different.
- **Infinite:** $f(x) \to \pm\infty$ near $x = a$ (a vertical asymptote).
:::

:::example Reading a graph
The graph above has three discontinuities. Classify each one.
@step At $x = -3$: from the left $f(x) \to 1$, from the right $f(x) \to -1.5$. The limit does not exist, so this is a **jump** discontinuity.
@step At $x = 0$: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = -1$ (the open circle) but $f(0) = 2$ (the dot). Condition 3 fails, so this is a **removable** discontinuity.
@step At $x = 3$: the graph has a vertical asymptote, so this is an **infinite** discontinuity.
@answer Jump at $x = -3$, removable at $x = 0$, infinite at $x = 3$.
:::

## Continuity of familiar functions

You do not need to check the three conditions for every function. These are continuous at every point of their domains:

- polynomials (everywhere);
- rational functions (everywhere except the zeros of the denominator);
- $\sqrt{x}$ (for $x \ge 0$), $e^x$, $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ (everywhere), and $\ln x$ (for $x > 0$);
- sums, products, quotients and compositions of continuous functions, wherever they are defined.

:::example Where is a rational function discontinuous?
Find the discontinuities of $f(x) = \dfrac{x - 3}{x^2 - x - 6}$.
@step Factor the denominator: $x^2 - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2)$, which is $0$ at $x = 3$ and $x = -2$.
@step At $x = 3$ the factor $(x - 3)$ cancels, so there is a hole: $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x + 2}$ for $x \ne 3$.
@step At $x = -2$ the numerator is $-5 \ne 0$: a vertical asymptote.
@answer $f$ is discontinuous at $x = 3$ (removable) and $x = -2$ (infinite).
:::

:::mistake A cancelled factor still counts
Cancelling $(x - 3)$ does not make $f(3)$ defined. The original function is still undefined at $x = 3$, so it is still discontinuous there.
:::

## Piecewise functions

A piecewise function built from continuous pieces can only be discontinuous where the formula changes. At each break, compare the left-hand limit, the right-hand limit and the function value.

:::example Checking a break
Is $f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 + 1, & x < 1 \\ 3x - 1, & x \ge 1 \end{cases}$ continuous at $x = 1$?
@step $f(1) = 3(1) - 1 = 2$ (the second piece includes $x = 1$).
@step $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1^2 + 1 = 2$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 3(1) - 1 = 2$.
@answer Both one-sided limits equal $f(1) = 2$, so $f$ is continuous at $x = 1$.
:::

To make a function continuous, choose the constant so that the pieces meet.

:::example Finding k
Find $k$ so that $f(x) = \begin{cases} kx - 1, & x < 2 \\ x^2 + 3, & x \ge 2 \end{cases}$ is continuous.
@step The left piece approaches $2k - 1$ as $x \to 2^-$.
@step The right piece gives $2^2 + 3 = 7$.
@step Set them equal: $2k - 1 = 7$.
@answer $k = 4$
:::

:::example Filling a hole
Find $c$ so that $f(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^2 - x - 6}{x - 3}, & x \ne 3 \\ c, & x = 3 \end{cases}$ is continuous at $x = 3$.
@step For $x \ne 3$: $\dfrac{(x - 3)(x + 2)}{x - 3} = x + 2$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = 5$.
@step For continuity, $f(3) = c$ must equal the limit.
@answer $c = 5$
:::

## Continuity on an interval

A function is **continuous on an interval** if it is continuous at every point of the interval (using one-sided limits at a closed endpoint). Continuous functions have a useful property: they cannot skip values.

:::definition Intermediate Value Theorem
If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $N$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c) = N$ for at least one $c$ in $(a, b)$.
:::

:::example Locating a root
Show that $x^3 + x - 1 = 0$ has a solution between $x = 0$ and $x = 1$.
@step $f(x) = x^3 + x - 1$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $[0, 1]$.
@step $f(0) = -1 < 0$ and $f(1) = 1 > 0$, so $0$ is between $f(0)$ and $f(1)$.
@answer By the Intermediate Value Theorem, $f(c) = 0$ for some $c$ in $(0, 1)$.
:::

:::tip Why continuity matters
Without continuity the theorem fails: $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is negative at $x = -1$ and positive at $x = 1$, but it is never $0$. The graph jumps across the $x$-axis at its asymptote.

:::summary
- $f$ is continuous at $a$ when $f(a)$ is defined, $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists, and they are equal.
- Discontinuities are removable (a hole), jump (different one-sided limits) or infinite (a vertical asymptote).
- Polynomials, rational functions, roots, exponentials, logarithms and sine and cosine are continuous on their domains.
- For a piecewise function, check each break; set the pieces equal to find a constant that makes it continuous.
- The Intermediate Value Theorem: a function continuous on $[a, b]$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.
:::
