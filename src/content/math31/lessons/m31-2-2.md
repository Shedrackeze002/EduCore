Graphs and tables suggest the value of a limit; algebra proves it. This lesson collects the algebraic methods you will use again and again — they are exactly the steps needed to find derivatives in Unit 3.

## Direct substitution

Limits obey the arithmetic you expect: the limit of a sum, product or quotient is the sum, product or quotient of the limits (as long as you never divide by a limit of $0$). For polynomials and rational functions this gives a shortcut.

:::definition Direct substitution
If $f$ is a polynomial, or a rational function with $a$ in its domain, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.
:::

:::example Substitute when you can
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} (3x^2 - x + 1)$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{2x + 5}{x - 3}$.
@step The polynomial: $3(2)^2 - 2 + 1 = 11$.
@step The rational function: the denominator at $x = -1$ is $-4 \ne 0$, so substitute: $\dfrac{2(-1) + 5}{-1 - 3} = \dfrac{3}{-4}$.
@answer $11$ and $-\dfrac{3}{4}$
:::

## The form 0/0: factor and cancel

:::definition Indeterminate form
An expression such as $\frac{0}{0}$ that gives no information about a limit. When substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, the limit might be any number, or might not exist — more work is needed.
:::

If substituting $x = a$ into a rational function gives $\frac{0}{0}$, then $(x - a)$ is a factor of both the numerator and the denominator. Cancel it: the simplified function agrees with the original everywhere except at $x = a$, and the limit ignores $x = a$.

:::example Cancelling a common factor
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$.
@step Substituting gives $\frac{0}{0}$.
@step $\dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} = \dfrac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{x - 3} = x + 3$ for $x \ne 3$.
@step $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3} (x + 3) = 6$
@answer $6$
:::

:::example Two trinomials
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 + x - 6}{x^2 - 4}$.
@step Substituting gives $\frac{0}{0}$.
@step $\dfrac{(x + 3)(x - 2)}{(x + 2)(x - 2)} = \dfrac{x + 3}{x + 2}$ for $x \ne 2$.
@answer $\dfrac{2 + 3}{2 + 2} = \dfrac{5}{4}$
:::

:::example Combining fractions first
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{2}}{x - 2}$.
@step Substituting gives $\frac{0}{0}$. Combine the numerator: $\dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{2 - x}{2x} = \dfrac{-(x - 2)}{2x}$.
@step Divide by $x - 2$: the expression equals $\dfrac{-1}{2x}$ for $x \ne 2$.
@answer $\dfrac{-1}{2(2)} = -\dfrac{1}{4}$
:::

:::mistake 0/0 is not an answer
$\frac{0}{0}$ is not $0$, not $1$ and not "does not exist". It is a signal to simplify.
:::

## Rationalizing

When a square root causes the $\frac{0}{0}$, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the **conjugate**. The product $(\sqrt{A} - B)(\sqrt{A} + B) = A - B^2$ removes the root.

:::example Multiplying by the conjugate
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 9} - 3}{x}$.
@step Substituting gives $\frac{0}{0}$.
@step $\dfrac{\sqrt{x + 9} - 3}{x} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{x + 9} + 3}{\sqrt{x + 9} + 3} = \dfrac{(x + 9) - 9}{x(\sqrt{x + 9} + 3)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x + 9} + 3}$
@step $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + 9} + 3} = \frac{1}{3 + 3}$
@answer $\dfrac{1}{6}$
:::

:::tip Leave the denominator factored
After multiplying by the conjugate, do not expand the denominator. You want to see the factor that cancels.
:::

## The special limit sin θ / θ

Substituting $\theta = 0$ into $\dfrac{\sin \theta}{\theta}$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$, and no algebra cancels it. In section 2.1, a table suggested the limit is $1$; here is why.

For $0 < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$, compare three areas in a unit circle: a triangle inside the sector, the sector, and a triangle containing the sector. Their areas are $\frac{1}{2}\sin\theta \le \frac{1}{2}\theta \le \frac{1}{2}\tan\theta$. Dividing by $\frac{1}{2}\sin\theta$ and taking reciprocals gives

$$\cos\theta \le \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \le 1.$$

As $\theta \to 0$, $\cos\theta \to 1$, so $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}$ is squeezed to $1$ (the same holds for $\theta < 0$).

$$\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1 \quad (\theta \text{ in radians})$$

To use it, make the denominator match the angle inside the sine.

:::example Adjusting the angle
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 5x}{x}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 4x}{\sin 3x}$.
@step $\dfrac{\sin 5x}{x} = 5 \cdot \dfrac{\sin 5x}{5x} \to 5(1) = 5$
@step $\dfrac{\sin 4x}{\sin 3x} = \dfrac{4 \cdot \frac{\sin 4x}{4x}}{3 \cdot \frac{\sin 3x}{3x}} \to \dfrac{4(1)}{3(1)}$
@answer $5$ and $\dfrac{4}{3}$
:::

:::mistake Cancelling inside a sine
$\dfrac{\sin 5x}{x}$ is not $\sin 5$: the $x$ is inside the function and cannot be cancelled.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Try direct substitution first. A non-zero denominator means you are done.
- $\frac{0}{0}$ is an indeterminate form: factor and cancel, combine fractions, or multiply by a conjugate, then substitute.
- $\displaystyle\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$ in radians. Rewrite $\dfrac{\sin kx}{x}$ as $k \cdot \dfrac{\sin kx}{kx}$.
:::
