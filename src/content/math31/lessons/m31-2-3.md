Two new questions about limits: what happens when a function's values grow without bound near a point, and what happens to $f(x)$ as $x$ itself grows without bound? The answers describe the asymptotes of a graph exactly.

## Infinite limits and vertical asymptotes

:::definition Infinite limit
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \infty$ means $f(x)$ becomes larger than any number we choose when $x$ is close enough to $a$ on the right. Limits equal to $-\infty$, from the left, or from both sides are defined the same way.
:::

$\infty$ is not a number. Writing $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \infty$ is a precise way of saying the limit does not exist **because** the values grow without bound. If $f(x) \to \pm\infty$ as $x \to a^+$ or $x \to a^-$, the line $x = a$ is a **vertical asymptote**.

The graph of $y = \dfrac{1}{x - 2} + 1$ shows both kinds of behaviour: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = \infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = -\infty$.

To find an infinite limit without a graph, check that the numerator approaches a non-zero number while the denominator approaches $0$, then work out the **sign**.

:::example Using signs
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{x + 1}{x - 3}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{3}{(x + 1)^2}$.
@step As $x \to 3^-$, the numerator approaches $4$ (positive) and $x - 3$ is a small negative number. Positive ÷ small negative is large and negative.
@step As $x \to -1$ from either side, $(x + 1)^2$ is a small positive number, and $3$ is positive.
@answer $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{x + 1}{x - 3} = -\infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{3}{(x + 1)^2} = \infty$
:::

:::mistake 0/0 is not a vertical asymptote
A vertical asymptote needs a non-zero number over $0$. If both the numerator and the denominator approach $0$, simplify first: you may find a hole instead.
:::

## Limits at infinity

:::definition Limit at infinity
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ means $f(x)$ gets as close to $L$ as we like for all large enough $x$; $x \to -\infty$ is similar. Then $y = L$ is a **horizontal asymptote**.
:::

The key fact is that $\dfrac{c}{x^n} \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$ for any constant $c$ and $n > 0$. So to find the limit of a rational function, divide every term by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator.

:::example Equal degrees
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{2x^2 + 7}$.
@step Divide every term by $x^2$: $\dfrac{3 - \frac{5}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \frac{7}{x^2}}$
@step As $x \to \infty$, each fraction with $x$ in the denominator approaches $0$.
@answer $\dfrac{3}{2}$, so $y = \dfrac{3}{2}$ is a horizontal asymptote.
:::

:::example A lower-degree numerator
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{4x + 1}{x^2 - 3}$.
@step Divide by $x^2$: $\dfrac{\frac{4}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}}{1 - \frac{3}{x^2}}$
@step The numerator approaches $0$ and the denominator approaches $1$.
@answer $0$
:::

:::

## Comparing degrees

The examples follow a pattern for $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$:

- degree of $P$ < degree of $Q$: the limit is $0$;
- equal degrees: the limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients;
- degree of $P$ > degree of $Q$: the limit is $\infty$ or $-\infty$ (no horizontal asymptote). Find the sign from the leading terms.

:::example A higher-degree numerator
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{2x^3 - x}{x^2 + 4}$.
@step For large $|x|$, the function behaves like $\dfrac{2x^3}{x^2} = 2x$.
@answer As $x \to -\infty$, $2x \to -\infty$, so the limit is $-\infty$.
:::

Roots need one more idea: $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, which equals $-x$ when $x < 0$.

:::example A root at negative infinity
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{9x^2 + 1}}{2x - 5}$.
@step For $x < 0$, $x = -\sqrt{x^2}$. Divide the numerator by $-\sqrt{x^2}$ and the denominator by $x$.
@step $\dfrac{-\sqrt{9 + \frac{1}{x^2}}}{2 - \frac{5}{x}} \to \dfrac{-\sqrt{9}}{2}$
@answer $-\dfrac{3}{2}$
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the sign
Dividing $\sqrt{9x^2 + 1}$ by $x$ as if $\sqrt{x^2} = x$ gives $+\frac{3}{2}$ at $-\infty$. For negative $x$, the numerator is positive and the denominator is negative, so the answer must be negative.
:::

:::check m31-limit-infinity 2
:::

## The asymptotes of a rational function

:::example Putting it together
Describe the asymptotes and holes of $f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 - 2x - 4}{x^2 - 4}$.
@step Factor: $f(x) = \dfrac{2(x - 2)(x + 1)}{(x - 2)(x + 2)} = \dfrac{2(x + 1)}{x + 2}$ for $x \ne 2$.
@step At $x = 2$ the factor cancels, so there is a hole at $\left(2, \frac{2(3)}{4}\right) = (2, 1.5)$.
@step At $x = -2$ the denominator is $0$ and the numerator is $-2$: a vertical asymptote. As $x \to -2^+$, $f(x) \to -\infty$; as $x \to -2^-$, $f(x) \to \infty$.
@step The degrees are equal, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \frac{2}{1} = 2$.
@answer A hole at $(2, 1.5)$, a vertical asymptote $x = -2$ and a horizontal asymptote $y = 2$.
:::

:::summary
- Non-zero ÷ (something approaching $0$) gives an infinite limit; decide the sign from each factor. Then $x = a$ is a vertical asymptote.
- To find a limit at $\pm\infty$, divide by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator; $\frac{c}{x^n} \to 0$.
- Compare degrees: lower on top gives $0$, equal gives the ratio of leading coefficients, higher on top gives $\pm\infty$.
- With roots at $-\infty$, remember $\sqrt{x^2} = |x| = -x$.
:::
