How long is the diagonal of a TV screen? How far is it straight across a soccer field? You can find lengths like these without measuring them, using a rule about right triangles that is more than 2500 years old.

## Parts of a right triangle

A **right triangle** has one $90^\circ$ angle. A small square in the corner marks the right angle.

:::figure

:::

:::definition Legs and hypotenuse
The two sides that make the right angle are the **legs**. The side across from the right angle is the **hypotenuse**. The hypotenuse is always the **longest** side.
:::

:::mistake The hypotenuse can be anywhere
A triangle can be turned or flipped. Do not just pick the side that looks slanted. Find the right-angle box, then look straight across from it.
:::

## Squares on the sides

Draw a square on each side of a right triangle with legs of 3 units and 4 units. Count the unit squares.

:::

The squares on the legs have areas $3^2 = 9$ and $4^2 = 16$. The square on the hypotenuse has area $5^2 = 25$. Notice that $9 + 16 = 25$. This works for every right triangle.

:::definition Pythagorean theorem
In a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$,
$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$
The areas of the squares on the two legs add up to the area of the square on the hypotenuse.
:::

## Finding the hypotenuse

Square the two legs, add, then take the square root.

:::example Whole-number answer
A right triangle has legs of $6\ \text{cm}$ and $8\ \text{cm}$. How long is the hypotenuse?
@step $c^2 = 6^2 + 8^2$
@step $c^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$
@step $c = \sqrt{100} = 10$
@answer The hypotenuse is $10\ \text{cm}$ long.
:::

Most answers are not whole numbers. Use the $\sqrt{\ }$ key on a calculator, and round only at the **end**.

:::example Rounding the answer
A right triangle has legs of $5\ \text{m}$ and $9\ \text{m}$. Find the hypotenuse to the nearest tenth of a metre.
@step $c^2 = 5^2 + 9^2 = 25 + 81 = 106$
@step $c = \sqrt{106} \approx 10.2956$
@answer The hypotenuse is about $10.3\ \text{m}$ long.
:::

:::tip Does your answer make sense?
The hypotenuse must be longer than each leg. Here, $10.3$ is longer than $9$, so the answer is reasonable.
:::

## Finding a leg

When you know the hypotenuse and one leg, subtract instead of adding:

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \quad \text{so} \quad b^2 = c^2 - a^2$$

:::example A whole-number leg
The hypotenuse of a right triangle is $13\ \text{ft}$ and one leg is $5\ \text{ft}$. Find the other leg, $x$.
@step $5^2 + x^2 = 13^2$
@step $x^2 = 13^2 - 5^2 = 169 - 25 = 144$
@step $x = \sqrt{144} = 12$
@answer The other leg is $12\ \text{ft}$ long.
:::

:::example A leg with rounding
The hypotenuse is $15\ \text{cm}$ and one leg is $9.5\ \text{cm}$. Find the other leg to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.
@step $x^2 = 15^2 - 9.5^2 = 225 - 90.25 = 134.75$
@step $x = \sqrt{134.75} \approx 11.608$
@answer The other leg is about $11.6\ \text{cm}$ long.
:::

:::mistake Adding when you should subtract
If you add the squares when you are finding a **leg**, your answer will be longer than the hypotenuse. That is impossible. A leg is always shorter than the hypotenuse.
:::

:::summary
- The legs make the right angle. The hypotenuse is across from it and is the longest side.
- Pythagorean theorem: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
- Hypotenuse: square the legs, **add**, take the square root.
- Leg: square both sides you know, **subtract**, take the square root.
- Round only at the end, and check that your answer makes sense.
:::
