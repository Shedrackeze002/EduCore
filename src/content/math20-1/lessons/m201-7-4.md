A 12 V battery pushes a current of $I = \dfrac{12}{R}$ amperes through a resistance of $R$ ohms. Double the resistance and the current is halved; let the resistance shrink toward $0$ and the current shoots up. That is exactly how a **reciprocal function** behaves.

## The reciprocal of a function

:::definition Reciprocal function
For a function $y = f(x)$, its reciprocal function is $y = \dfrac{1}{f(x)}$. Each $y$-value is replaced by its reciprocal: the point $(x, y)$ becomes $\left(x, \dfrac{1}{y}\right)$.
:::

Here is what happens to $f(x) = x - 2$:

| $x$ | $0$ | $1$ | $1.5$ | $1.9$ | $2$ | $2.1$ | $3$ | $4$ | $12$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $f(x)$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $-0.5$ | $-0.1$ | $0$ | $0.1$ | $1$ | $2$ | $10$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$ | $-0.5$ | $-1$ | $-2$ | $-10$ | undefined | $10$ | $1$ | $0.5$ | $0.1$ |

Small values of $f$ become huge values of $\dfrac{1}{f}$, huge values become tiny ones, and the sign never changes.

:::definition Asymptote
A line that a graph approaches more and more closely. Where $f(x) = 0$, $y = \dfrac{1}{f(x)}$ has a **vertical asymptote**; as $|f(x)|$ grows without bound, $\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$ approaches the **horizontal asymptote** $y = 0$.
:::

:::definition Invariant points
Points that are on both $y = f(x)$ and $y = \dfrac{1}{f(x)}$. They occur where $f(x) = 1$ or $f(x) = -1$, because $1$ and $-1$ are their own reciprocals.
:::

:::summary
- $y = \dfrac{1}{f(x)}$ replaces each $y$-value with its reciprocal and keeps its sign.
- Zeros of $f$ become vertical asymptotes; $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote.
- Invariant points are where $f(x) = 1$ or $f(x) = -1$.
- A maximum or minimum $(p, q)$ of $f$ (with $q \ne 0$) becomes a minimum or maximum $\left(p, \tfrac{1}{q}\right)$.
- Quadratics with two, one or no zeros give three, two or one branch.
:::
