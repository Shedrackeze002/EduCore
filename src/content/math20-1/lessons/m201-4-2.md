A quadratic equation asks one question: for which values of $x$ is $ax^2 + bx + c$ equal to zero? You can answer it from a graph, where the answers are the $x$-intercepts, or with algebra, by factoring.

## Roots, zeros and x-intercepts

:::definition Quadratic equation
An equation that can be written in **standard form** $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, where $a \ne 0$.
:::

:::definition Roots, zeros and x-intercepts
The **roots** of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ are its solutions. They are the **zeros** of the function $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ (the inputs that give $f(x) = 0$) and the **$x$-intercepts** of the graph of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$.
:::

All three words describe the same numbers. On the graph of $y = x^2 - 2x - 3$, the parabola crosses the $x$-axis at $x = -1$ and $x = 3$, so those are the roots of $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$.

## The zero product property

:::definition Zero product property
If $AB = 0$, then $A = 0$ or $B = 0$.
:::

Why is it true? If $A \ne 0$, divide both sides of $AB = 0$ by $A$ to get $B = 0$. So at least one factor must be zero. This is why factoring solves equations: once one side is a product and the other side is $0$, each factor gives a root.

:::example Solving by factoring
Solve $2x^2 + 5x - 12 = 0$.
@step $ac = -24$, sum $5$: $8$ and $-3$. $2x^2 + 8x - 3x - 12 = 2x(x + 4) - 3(x + 4)$
@step $(2x - 3)(x + 4) = 0$
@step $2x - 3 = 0$ or $x + 4 = 0$
@answer $x = \frac{3}{2}$ or $x = -4$
:::

:::mistake The other side must be zero
$(x - 2)(x + 3) = 6$ does **not** mean $x - 2 = 6$ or $x + 3 = 6$. Two numbers can multiply to 6 in many ways. Expand first: $x^2 + x - 6 = 6$, so $x^2 + x - 12 = 0$, $(x + 4)(x - 3) = 0$ and $x = -4$ or $x = 3$.
:::

:::mistake Never divide by the variable
Dividing $3x^2 = 12x$ by $x$ gives only $x = 4$ and loses a root. Instead, $3x^2 - 12x = 0$, so $3x(x - 4) = 0$ and $x = 0$ or $x = 4$.
:::

## Standard form first

1. Expand and collect every term on one side, so the equation reads $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.
2. Factor completely.
3. Set each factor equal to zero and solve.
4. Check each root in the **original** equation.

:::example Rearranging
Solve $x(x + 2) = 15$.
@step Expand and subtract 15: $x^2 + 2x - 15 = 0$
@step Factor: $(x + 5)(x - 3) = 0$
@step Check: $3(5) = 15$ ✓ and $(-5)(-3) = 15$ ✓
@answer $x = -5$ or $x = 3$
:::

:::example A known root
One root of $x^2 + kx - 12 = 0$ is $x = 3$. Find $k$ and the other root.
@step A root makes the equation true: $3^2 + 3k - 12 = 0$, so $3k = 3$ and $k = 1$.
@step $x^2 + x - 12 = (x + 4)(x - 3) = 0$
@answer $k = 1$; the other root is $x = -4$.
:::

## How many roots?

A parabola can cross the $x$-axis twice, touch it once at its vertex, or miss it completely. So a quadratic equation has **two distinct real roots**, **one real root** (two equal roots), or **no real roots**.

:::figure

:::

The graph of $y = x^2 - 4x + 6$ has its vertex at $(2, 2)$ and opens up, so it never reaches the $x$-axis: $x^2 - 4x + 6 = 0$ has no real roots.

:::summary
- The roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ are the zeros of $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ and the $x$-intercepts of its graph.
- Zero product property: if $AB = 0$, then $A = 0$ or $B = 0$. One side must be $0$ before you use it.
- Write the equation in standard form, factor, solve each factor, and check in the original equation.
- A quadratic equation has two, one or no real roots, just as a parabola crosses, touches or misses the $x$-axis.
:::
