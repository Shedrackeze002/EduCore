A graph shows how many solutions a system has, but exact answers, especially fractions and irrational values, come from algebra. Substitution and elimination, the methods you used for linear systems, work here too.

## Substitution for linear-quadratic systems

:::definition Substitution
Solving one equation for a variable and replacing that variable in the other equation, which leaves one equation in one variable.
:::

1. Solve the linear equation for $y$ (if it is not already).
2. Substitute into the quadratic equation and collect all terms on one side.
3. Solve the quadratic equation for $x$.
4. Substitute each $x$-value into the **linear** equation to find $y$.

:::example Substitution
Solve $y = x^2 - 4x + 1$ and $y = 2x - 7$.
@step Substitute: $x^2 - 4x + 1 = 2x - 7$, so $x^2 - 6x + 8 = 0$.
@step Factor: $(x - 2)(x - 4) = 0$, so $x = 2$ or $x = 4$.
@step $y = 2(2) - 7 = -3$ and $y = 2(4) - 7 = 1$.
@answer $(2, -3)$ and $(4, 1)$
:::

:::example A line in general form
Solve $y = 2x^2 + 6x - 2$ and $x - y = -1$.
@step Solve the linear equation for $y$: $y = x + 1$.
@step Substitute: $2x^2 + 6x - 2 = x + 1$, so $2x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0$.
@step Factor: $(2x - 1)(x + 3) = 0$, so $x = \frac{1}{2}$ or $x = -3$.
@step $y = \frac{1}{2} + 1 = \frac{3}{2}$ and $y = -3 + 1 = -2$.
@answer $(-3, -2)$ and $\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$
:::

## One solution or no solution

The discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ of the quadratic equation you get after substituting tells you how many solutions the system has:

- positive: two solutions (the line crosses the parabola twice);
- zero: one solution (the line is tangent to the parabola);
- negative: no solution (the line misses the parabola).

:::example A tangent line
Solve $y = x^2 + 3$ and $y = 2x + 2$.
@step Substitute: $x^2 + 3 = 2x + 2$, so $x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$.
@step Discriminant: $(-2)^2 - 4(1)(1) = 0$, so there is exactly one solution.
@step $(x - 1)^2 = 0$, so $x = 1$ and $y = 2(1) + 2 = 4$.
@answer One solution: $(1, 4)$.
:::

If the line were $y = x$ instead, substituting would give $x^2 - x + 3 = 0$, with discriminant $1 - 12 = -11 < 0$: no solution.

## Elimination for quadratic-quadratic systems

:::definition Elimination
Adding or subtracting the equations so that one variable cancels.
:::

When both equations are solved for $y$, subtract one from the other: $y$ cancels and a single equation in $x$ is left.

:::example Elimination
Solve $y = 2x^2 + x - 3$ and $y = x^2 + 4x + 1$.
@step Subtract the second equation from the first: $0 = (2x^2 + x - 3) - (x^2 + 4x + 1) = x^2 - 3x - 4$.
@step Factor: $(x - 4)(x + 1) = 0$, so $x = 4$ or $x = -1$.
@step In $y = x^2 + 4x + 1$: $x = -1$ gives $y = -2$, and $x = 4$ gives $y = 33$.
@answer $(-1, -2)$ and $(4, 33)$
:::

:::mistake Subtracting only the first term
When you subtract a whole equation, the minus sign applies to **every** term: $-(x^2 + 4x + 1) = -x^2 - 4x - 1$.
:::

:::tip When the x² terms cancel
If both parabolas have the same value of $a$, subtracting leaves a linear equation, so there is at most one solution. If the $x$-terms cancel too and only a non-zero constant is left, there is no solution.
:::

## Solving problems with systems

Define the variables, write one equation for each fact, solve, and check that each answer makes sense in context.

:::example Two numbers
Two positive numbers have a sum of $13$, and the sum of their squares is $89$. Find the numbers.
@step $x + y = 13$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 89$. From the first equation, $y = 13 - x$.
@step $x^2 + (13 - x)^2 = 89$, so $2x^2 - 26x + 80 = 0$, or $x^2 - 13x + 40 = 0$.
@step $(x - 5)(x - 8) = 0$, so $x = 5$ (then $y = 8$) or $x = 8$ (then $y = 5$).
@answer The numbers are $5$ and $8$. Check: $25 + 64 = 89$ ✓
:::

:::example A rocket and a drone
A model rocket's height is $h = -5t^2 + 30t + 1$ and a drone's height is $h = 5t + 21$, both in metres after $t$ seconds. When are they at the same height?
@step $-5t^2 + 30t + 1 = 5t + 21$, so $-5t^2 + 25t - 20 = 0$.
@step Divide by $-5$: $t^2 - 5t + 4 = 0$, so $(t - 1)(t - 4) = 0$.
@answer At $t = 1$ s (both at $26$ m) and at $t = 4$ s (both at $41$ m).
:::

:::tip Reject answers that do not fit
A negative time, a negative length or a point behind the starting position can be a correct root of the equation but not an answer to the problem.
:::

:::summary
- Linear-quadratic: solve the linear equation for $y$, substitute into the quadratic, and solve the resulting quadratic equation.
- Quadratic-quadratic: subtract the equations to eliminate $y$, then solve for $x$.
- Find each $y$ by substituting back into one of the original equations, and check both equations.
- The discriminant of the resulting quadratic gives the number of solutions: positive two, zero one, negative none.
:::
