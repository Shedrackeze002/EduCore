Many quadratic equations, such as $x^2 + 6x + 4 = 0$, cannot be factored over the integers. Two methods work for every quadratic equation: completing the square, and the quadratic formula that comes from it.

## Taking square roots

If one side is a perfect square, take the square root of both sides. A positive number has **two** square roots, so remember $\pm$.

$$(x - p)^2 = q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x - p = \pm\sqrt{q} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = p \pm \sqrt{q}$$

If $q < 0$ there are no real roots, because no real number squared is negative.

:::example Square roots of both sides
Solve (a) $(x + 3)^2 = 7$ (b) $(x - 1)^2 = 16$ (c) $(x - 5)^2 = -4$.
@step (a) $x + 3 = \pm\sqrt{7}$, so $x = -3 \pm \sqrt{7}$ (exact roots).
@step (b) $x - 1 = \pm 4$, so $x = 5$ or $x = -3$.
@step (c) A square cannot equal $-4$: no real roots.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting the negative root
$\sqrt{16} = 4$, but the equation $(x - 1)^2 = 16$ has two solutions because $(-4)^2 = 16$ as well. Write $\pm$ as soon as you take the square root of both sides.
:::

## Completing the square

To solve $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ by completing the square:

1. Divide every term by $a$ (if $a \ne 1$).
2. Move the constant term to the right side.
3. Add $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ — the square of half the coefficient of $x$ — to both sides.
4. Write the left side as a square, take square roots ($\pm$) and solve.

:::example When a = 1
Solve $x^2 + 6x + 4 = 0$ by completing the square.
@step $x^2 + 6x = -4$
@step Half of 6 is 3 and $3^2 = 9$: $x^2 + 6x + 9 = -4 + 9 = 5$
@step $(x + 3)^2 = 5$, so $x + 3 = \pm\sqrt{5}$
@answer $x = -3 \pm \sqrt{5}$ (about $-0.76$ and $-5.24$)
:::

:::example When a ≠ 1
Solve $2x^2 - 8x + 3 = 0$ by completing the square.
@step Divide by 2: $x^2 - 4x + \frac{3}{2} = 0$, so $x^2 - 4x = -\frac{3}{2}$
@step Add $(-2)^2 = 4$: $x^2 - 4x + 4 = \frac{5}{2}$, so $(x - 2)^2 = \frac{5}{2}$
@step $x - 2 = \pm\sqrt{\frac{5}{2}} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2}$
@answer $x = 2 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2} = \dfrac{4 \pm \sqrt{10}}{2}$
:::

## Deriving the quadratic formula

Complete the square on $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with letters instead of numbers. This proof shows why the formula always works.

:::example Proof of the quadratic formula
Solve $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, where $a \ne 0$.
@step Divide by $a$ and move the constant: $x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x = -\dfrac{c}{a}$
@step Add $\left(\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}$ to both sides: $\left(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2} - \dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$
@step Take square roots: $x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$ (the $\pm$ covers the sign of $\sqrt{4a^2} = 2|a|$)
@answer $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$
:::

:::definition Quadratic formula
The roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, $a \ne 0$, are
$$x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$
:::

## Using the quadratic formula

Write the equation in standard form, identify $a$, $b$ and $c$ with their signs, and substitute. Simplify the radical for exact roots, or use a calculator for decimal approximations.

:::example Exact roots
Solve $3x^2 - 4x - 2 = 0$. Give exact roots.
@step $a = 3$, $b = -4$, $c = -2$
@step $x = \dfrac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(3)(-2)}}{2(3)} = \dfrac{4 \pm \sqrt{40}}{6}$
@step $\sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}$, so $x = \dfrac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{10}}{6}$
@answer $x = \dfrac{2 \pm \sqrt{10}}{3}$
:::

:::example Decimal roots
Solve $2x^2 + 5x - 1 = 0$. Round to the nearest hundredth.
@step $b^2 - 4ac = 25 - 4(2)(-1) = 33$
@step $x = \dfrac{-5 \pm \sqrt{33}}{4}$
@answer $x \approx 0.19$ or $x \approx -2.69$
:::

:::mistake Two common slips
When $b$ is negative, $-b$ is positive: for $b = -4$, $-b = 4$ and $b^2 = 16$. And the denominator $2a$ divides the **whole** numerator, not just the radical.
:::

:::tip Typing exact roots
Type $\dfrac{2 \pm \sqrt{10}}{3}$ as two answers: `(2 + sqrt(10))/3, (2 - sqrt(10))/3`.
:::

:::summary
- $(x - p)^2 = q$ gives $x = p \pm \sqrt{q}$ when $q \ge 0$, and no real roots when $q < 0$.
- Completing the square: divide by $a$, move the constant, add $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$, then take square roots.
- Quadratic formula: $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$, proved by completing the square.
- Simplify radicals for exact roots; round only at the end for decimal roots.
:::
