Estimates are useful, but carpenters, machinists and scientists need precise measurements. In this lesson you will read metric and imperial rulers accurately, use a vernier caliper to measure to a tenth of a millimetre, and develop strategies for measuring lengths that are not straight.

## Precision

:::definition Precision
The **precision** of a measurement depends on the smallest unit on the instrument. A ruler marked in millimetres is more precise than one marked only in centimetres.
:::

A measurement of $7.4\ \text{cm}$ (to the nearest millimetre) is more precise than $7\ \text{cm}$ (to the nearest centimetre). The more precise the instrument, the smaller the possible error.

## Reading a metric ruler

On a metric ruler, the numbered marks are centimetres and each small division is $1\ \text{mm} = 0.1\ \text{cm}$.

:::figure
{"type":"ruler","system":"metric","length":10,"start":0,"end":6.3}
:::

The bar above starts at 0 and ends 3 small divisions past the 6, so its length is $6.3\ \text{cm} = 63\ \text{mm}$.

If an object does not start at 0, **subtract** the starting reading from the ending reading.

:::example An object that does not start at zero
A bar starts at the $1.8\ \text{cm}$ mark and ends at the $9.5\ \text{cm}$ mark. How long is it?
@step Length = end − start
@step $9.5 - 1.8 = 7.7$
@answer The bar is $7.7\ \text{cm}$ ($77\ \text{mm}$) long.
:::

## Reading an imperial ruler

An imperial ruler divides each inch into halves, quarters, eighths and often sixteenths. The marks get shorter as the divisions get smaller:

- the longest marks are whole inches,
- the next longest is the $\tfrac12$ inch mark,
- then the $\tfrac14$ marks, then the $\tfrac18$ marks, and the shortest are the $\tfrac{1}{16}$ marks.

:::figure
{"type":"ruler","system":"imperial","length":4,"start":0,"end":2.6875,"division":16}
:::

To read a length: count the whole inches, then count the small spaces past the last whole inch, and write the fraction in lowest terms.

:::example Reading to the nearest sixteenth
The bar above ends 11 sixteenths past the 2-inch mark. What is its length?
@step Whole inches: 2
@step Extra: $\tfrac{11}{16}$ in (this fraction is already in lowest terms)
@answer The length is $2\tfrac{11}{16}$ in.
:::

:::tip Simplify the fraction
If a bar ends 12 sixteenths past the 3-inch mark, the length is $3\tfrac{12}{16} = 3\tfrac34$ in. Carpenters always say “three and three-quarter inches”, not “three and twelve-sixteenths”.
:::

## The vernier caliper

A **vernier caliper** measures lengths, outside diameters and inside diameters to the nearest $0.1\ \text{mm}$. It has a fixed **main scale** in millimetres and a sliding **vernier scale** with 10 divisions.

To read a vernier caliper:

1. Read the main scale at the **0 of the vernier scale**. Record the whole number of millimetres just before it.
2. Look along the vernier scale for the **one mark that lines up exactly** with a main-scale mark. That vernier number is the number of tenths of a millimetre.
3. Add the two readings.

:::figure
{"type":"caliper","reading":23.7}
:::

:::example Reading a vernier caliper
The vernier 0 is between 23 mm and 24 mm on the main scale, and the 7 on the vernier scale lines up exactly with a main-scale mark.
@step Main scale: $23\ \text{mm}$
@step Vernier: $7 \times 0.1 = 0.7\ \text{mm}$
@answer Reading: $23 + 0.7 = 23.7\ \text{mm}$ ($2.37\ \text{cm}$)
:::

:::mistake Reading the main scale at the wrong place
Always read the main scale at the **vernier zero**, not at the edge of the sliding jaw or at the vernier mark that lines up.
:::

## Measuring curved lengths

How would you measure the distance around a water bottle? A ruler cannot bend, so we need a **strategy**:

- **String method:** wrap a string (or strip of paper) around the object, mark it, then straighten it and measure with a ruler.
- **Wrap several times:** wrapping 5 times and dividing by 5 reduces the effect of small errors.
- **Use the diameter:** for a circle, measure the diameter with a caliper and calculate $C = \pi d$.

:::definition Circumference
The circumference $C$ of a circle with diameter $d$ is $C = \pi d$. So $d = \dfrac{C}{\pi}$.
:::

:::example Diameter from circumference
A string wrapped once around a tree trunk is $94\ \text{in}$ long. What is the diameter of the trunk?
@step $d = \dfrac{C}{\pi} = \dfrac{94}{\pi}$
@answer $d \approx 29.9\ \text{in}$
:::

:::example Wrapping several times
A thread wrapped 4 times around a pen is $142\ \text{mm}$ long. Find the pen’s diameter.
@step One circumference: $142 \div 4 = 35.5\ \text{mm}$
@step $d = 35.5 \div \pi$
@answer $d \approx 11.3\ \text{mm}$
:::

:::summary
- Precision depends on the smallest unit the instrument shows.
- Length = ending reading − starting reading.
- Imperial rulers are divided into $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac18$ and $\tfrac1{16}$ inch; write answers in lowest terms.
- Vernier caliper: main scale at the vernier 0, plus the aligned vernier mark in tenths of a millimetre.
- For curved lengths, use string, wrap several times, or use $C = \pi d$.
:::
