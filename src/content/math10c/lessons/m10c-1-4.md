A Canadian hockey player is listed at 6′1″; a marathon is 26.2 miles; a recipe from the U.S. measures a pan in inches. To move between the SI and imperial systems you use the same tool as before — conversion factors — with a few key relationships.

## Key relationships

| Imperial | SI |
|---|---|
| $1\ \text{in}$ | $= 2.54\ \text{cm}$ (exact) |
| $1\ \text{ft}$ | $= 30.48\ \text{cm} = 0.3048\ \text{m}$ (exact) |
| $1\ \text{yd}$ | $= 0.9144\ \text{m}$ (exact) |
| $1\ \text{mi}$ | $\approx 1.609\ \text{km}$ |

You only really need to remember $1\ \text{in} = 2.54\ \text{cm}$ — every other relationship can be built from it.

## Two methods

:::example Method 1: unit fractions
Convert $15\ \text{in}$ to centimetres.
@step Multiply by a fraction equal to 1 that has inches in the denominator: $15\ \text{in} \times \dfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}}$
@step The inches cancel.
@answer $= 38.1\ \text{cm}$
:::

:::example Method 2: proportion
Convert $100\ \text{m}$ to yards.
@step Set up equal ratios: $\dfrac{1\ \text{yd}}{0.9144\ \text{m}} = \dfrac{x}{100\ \text{m}}$
@step $x = \dfrac{100}{0.9144}$
@answer $x \approx 109.4\ \text{yd}$
:::

Both methods are proportional reasoning. Use whichever makes sense to you, but **always write the units** so you can check them.

## Multi-step conversions

Sometimes you need more than one conversion factor. Chain them so that each unit you do not want cancels.

:::example Height in feet and inches to centimetres
A basketball player is $6\ \text{ft}\ 4\ \text{in}$ tall. Find the height in centimetres.
@step Convert to inches: $6 \times 12 + 4 = 76\ \text{in}$
@step $76\ \text{in} \times \dfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}} = 193.04\ \text{cm}$
@answer about $193.0\ \text{cm}$
:::

:::example Converting a rate
A speed limit is $100\ \text{km/h}$. What is it in miles per hour?
@step Only the distance unit changes: $\dfrac{100\ \text{km}}{1\ \text{h}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mi}}{1.609\ \text{km}}$
@answer $\approx 62.2\ \text{mi/h}$
:::

:::example Two units changing at once
A runner covers $400\ \text{m}$ in $55\ \text{s}$. What is the speed in kilometres per hour?
@step $\dfrac{400\ \text{m}}{55\ \text{s}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{km}}{1000\ \text{m}} \times \dfrac{3600\ \text{s}}{1\ \text{h}}$
@step Metres cancel with metres and seconds cancel with seconds.
@answer $\approx 26.2\ \text{km/h}$
:::

## Is my answer reasonable?

Before trusting a calculator, estimate mentally with friendly numbers:

- $1\ \text{in} \approx 2.5\ \text{cm}$
- $1\ \text{ft} \approx 30\ \text{cm}$
- $1\ \text{m} \approx 3.3\ \text{ft}$ (a little more than 1 yd)
- $1\ \text{mi} \approx 1.6\ \text{km}$, and $1\ \text{km} \approx 0.6\ \text{mi}$

:::example Checking reasonableness
A student converted $26.2\ \text{mi}$ to $16.3\ \text{km}$. Is this reasonable?
@step A mile is **longer** than a kilometre, so the number of kilometres should be **larger** than 26.2.
@step Estimate: $26 \times 1.6 \approx 42$
@answer No. The student divided instead of multiplying. A marathon is about $42.2\ \text{km}$.
:::

:::mistake Multiplying when you should divide
If you convert to a smaller unit, the number must increase. If your answer goes the wrong way, flip your conversion fraction.
:::

:::summary
- $1\ \text{in} = 2.54\ \text{cm}$ (exact) is the key link between the systems.
- Use unit fractions or proportions; write units at every step.
- Chain conversion factors for multi-step and rate conversions.
- Check reasonableness with friendly approximations before you trust the calculator.
:::
