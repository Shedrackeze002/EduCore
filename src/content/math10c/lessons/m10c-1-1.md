How tall is your classroom door? How far is it from your home to school? You can answer questions like these without a tape measure if you have good **referents** — everyday objects whose lengths you already know. In this lesson you will learn the two systems of units used in Canada and how to estimate lengths in both.

## Two systems of measurement

Canada officially uses the **SI system** (the metric system), but the **imperial system** is still used every day: building materials are sold in feet and inches, people often give their height in feet and inches, and the United States uses imperial units for almost everything.

The SI system is based on powers of 10, which makes converting easy. The base unit of length is the **metre**.

| SI unit | Symbol | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| millimetre | mm | $1000\ \text{mm} = 1\ \text{m}$ |
| centimetre | cm | $100\ \text{cm} = 1\ \text{m}$, $10\ \text{mm} = 1\ \text{cm}$ |
| metre | m | base unit |
| kilometre | km | $1\ \text{km} = 1000\ \text{m}$ |

The imperial system grew out of body measurements, so its relationships are not powers of 10.

| Imperial unit | Symbol | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| inch | in or ″ | |
| foot | ft or ′ | $1\ \text{ft} = 12\ \text{in}$ |
| yard | yd | $1\ \text{yd} = 3\ \text{ft} = 36\ \text{in}$ |
| mile | mi | $1\ \text{mi} = 1760\ \text{yd} = 5280\ \text{ft}$ |

:::tip Reading the symbols
$5'\,7''$ means 5 feet 7 inches. The single mark ′ is feet and the double mark ″ is inches.
:::

## Referents

:::definition Referent
A **referent** is an object or distance that is approximately the size of one unit. You use it to estimate measurements without a measuring tool.
:::

Good referents are things you always have with you — parts of your body are perfect.

| Unit | A useful referent |
|---|---|
| 1 mm | the thickness of a dime |
| 1 cm | the width of your little fingernail |
| 1 m | the height of a doorknob above the floor, or one long stride |
| 1 km | the distance you walk in about 12 minutes |
| 1 in | the width of your thumb at the knuckle |
| 1 ft | the length of an adult’s shoe, or a sheet of paper (11 in) |
| 1 yd | from your nose to the fingertips of your outstretched arm |
| 1 mi | the distance you walk in about 20 minutes |

Referents also help you compare the two systems:

- $1\ \text{in}$ is about $2.5\ \text{cm}$ (exactly $2.54\ \text{cm}$), so an inch is a bit more than two fingernail widths.
- $1\ \text{m}$ is a little longer than $1\ \text{yd}$ ($1\ \text{yd} \approx 0.91\ \text{m}$).
- $1\ \text{ft}$ is about $30\ \text{cm}$ — about the length of a standard ruler.
- $1\ \text{mi}$ is about $1.6\ \text{km}$, so a mile is longer than a kilometre.

## Estimating with a referent

To estimate a length:

1. Choose a referent that is a sensible size for the object.
2. Count (or picture) how many times the referent fits along the length.
3. Multiply the number of referents by the size of one referent.

:::example Estimating the width of a classroom
Maya’s stride is about $0.75\ \text{m}$. She walks across her classroom in 12 strides. Estimate the width of the room.
@step Each stride is about $0.75\ \text{m}$.
@step $12 \times 0.75 = 9$
@answer The room is about $9\ \text{m}$ wide.
:::

:::example Estimating in imperial units
The distance from Jon’s nose to his outstretched fingertips is about 1 yard. A rug measures 4 of these lengths. Estimate the length of the rug in feet.
@step The rug is about $4\ \text{yd}$ long.
@step Since $1\ \text{yd} = 3\ \text{ft}$, $4\ \text{yd} = 4 \times 3 = 12\ \text{ft}$.
@answer The rug is about $12\ \text{ft}$ long.
:::

:::tip Estimates are not exact
An estimate gives a reasonable value, not the exact one. Two people with different stride lengths will get slightly different estimates — and that is fine. Always say “about” or use $\approx$.
:::

## Choosing an appropriate unit

The best unit gives a number that is easy to work with — not huge, not tiny. You should also match the units the situation uses (lumber in feet and inches, highway distances in kilometres).

:::example Justifying a choice of unit
Which unit would you use to measure (a) the thickness of a phone, (b) the length of a hockey rink, (c) the distance between two cities?
@step (a) A phone is less than 1 cm thick, so **millimetres** give a sensible whole number (about 8 mm).
@step (b) A rink is tens of metres long, so **metres** (about 60 m) or **feet** (about 200 ft) are sensible.
@step (c) Cities are far apart, so **kilometres** (or miles) avoid enormous numbers.
:::

:::mistake Using a unit that is far too small or too large
Saying a football field is about $91\,440\ \text{mm}$ long is technically correct but impractical. Choose the unit a person would actually use: about $100\ \text{yd}$ or $91\ \text{m}$.
:::

## Putting it together

:::example Comparing units with referents
Which is longer, $5\ \text{km}$ or $3\ \text{mi}$?
@step Use the referent relationship $1\ \text{mi} \approx 1.6\ \text{km}$.
@step $3\ \text{mi} \approx 3 \times 1.6 = 4.8\ \text{km}$.
@answer $5\ \text{km}$ is slightly longer than $3\ \text{mi}$.
:::

:::summary
- The **SI system** uses powers of 10: $10\ \text{mm} = 1\ \text{cm}$, $100\ \text{cm} = 1\ \text{m}$, $1000\ \text{m} = 1\ \text{km}$.
- The **imperial system**: $12\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{ft}$, $3\ \text{ft} = 1\ \text{yd}$, $5280\ \text{ft} = 1\ \text{mi}$.
- A **referent** is an everyday object about the size of one unit; use it to estimate.
- To estimate: choose a referent, count how many fit, and multiply.
- Choose a unit that gives a practical number and suits the context.
:::
