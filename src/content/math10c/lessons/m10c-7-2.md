If you know the slope of a line and **any** point on it — not necessarily the $y$-intercept — **slope-point form** lets you write its equation immediately.

## Where slope-point form comes from

Let $(x_1, y_1)$ be a known point on a line with slope $m$, and let $(x, y)$ be any other point on the line. By the slope formula:

$$\frac{y - y_1}{x - x_1} = m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$$

:::definition Slope-point form
$$y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$$
where $m$ is the slope and $(x_1, y_1)$ is a point on the line.
:::

:::example Reading slope-point form
State the slope and the point for $y + 3 = -2(x - 5)$.
@step $y + 3 = y - (-3)$, so $y_1 = -3$. And $x - 5$ gives $x_1 = 5$.
@answer $m = -2$, point $(5, -3)$
:::

:::mistake The signs flip
In $y - 4 = 3(x + 1)$, the point is $(-1, 4)$, not $(1, -4)$. The form has **minus** signs built in.
:::

## Writing equations

:::example Given a point and the slope
Write the equation of the line through $(2, -5)$ with slope $\tfrac34$, in slope-point form and in slope-intercept form.
@step Slope-point: $y + 5 = \tfrac34(x - 2)$
@step Expand: $y + 5 = \tfrac34 x - \tfrac32$
@step Subtract 5: $y = \tfrac34 x - \tfrac{13}{2}$
:::

:::example Given two points
Write the equation of the line through $(-1, 4)$ and $(3, -4)$.
@step Slope: $m = \dfrac{-4 - 4}{3 - (-1)} = \dfrac{-8}{4} = -2$
@step Use either point, say $(-1, 4)$: $y - 4 = -2(x + 1)$
@step Slope-intercept form: $y = -2x + 2$
:::

:::tip Either point works
Using $(3, -4)$ instead gives $y + 4 = -2(x - 3)$, which simplifies to the same $y = -2x + 2$. Different slope-point equations can describe the same line.
:::
