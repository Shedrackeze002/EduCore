Surface area tells you how much material covers an object. **Volume** tells you how much space is inside. In this lesson you will find the volume of prisms and cylinders, and discover why pyramids and cones hold exactly one third as much.

## Volume of prisms and cylinders

:::definition Volume
The **volume** of a 3-D object is the amount of space it occupies, measured in cubic units such as $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{ft}^3$.
:::

Think of a prism or cylinder as a stack of identical layers, each the shape of the base. If the base has area $B$ and the stack is $h$ high:

$$V = Bh \qquad (\text{area of base} \times \text{height})$$

- Rectangular prism: $V = lwh$
- Triangular prism: $V = \left(\tfrac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height of triangle}\right) \times \text{length}$
- Cylinder: $V = \pi r^2 h$

:::example A rectangular prism
A fish tank is $60\ \text{cm}$ long, $30\ \text{cm}$ wide and $40\ \text{cm}$ high. Find its volume and its capacity in litres.
@step $V = 60 \times 30 \times 40 = 72\,000\ \text{cm}^3$
@step Since $1000\ \text{cm}^3 = 1\ \text{L}$: $72\,000 \div 1000 = 72$
@answer $72\,000\ \text{cm}^3$, which holds $72\ \text{L}$
:::

:::example A cylinder
A can has radius $3.5\ \text{cm}$ and height $12\ \text{cm}$. Find its volume.
@step $V = \pi(3.5)^2(12) = 147\pi$
@answer $V \approx 461.8\ \text{cm}^3$
:::

:::example A triangular prism
A tent is a triangular prism. The triangular end has base $2.4\ \text{m}$ and height $1.5\ \text{m}$, and the tent is $3\ \text{m}$ long.
@step Base area: $\tfrac12(2.4)(1.5) = 1.8\ \text{m}^2$
@answer $V = 1.8 \times 3 = 5.4\ \text{m}^3$
:::

## Pyramids and cones: one third

Take a pyramid and a prism with the **same base and the same height**. Fill the pyramid with sand and pour it into the prism. It takes exactly **three** pyramids to fill the prism. The same is true for a cone and a cylinder.

$$V_{\text{pyramid}} = \tfrac13 Bh \qquad V_{\text{cone}} = \tfrac13 \pi r^2 h$$

:::example A square pyramid
Find the volume of a square pyramid with base side $9\ \text{m}$ and height $12\ \text{m}$.
@step $B = 9^2 = 81\ \text{m}^2$
@answer $V = \tfrac13(81)(12) = 324\ \text{m}^3$
:::

:::example Using the 1/3 relationship
A cylinder has a volume of $150\ \text{cm}^3$. A cone has the same base and height. What is the cone’s volume?
@answer $V = \tfrac13(150) = 50\ \text{cm}^3$
:::

:::example A cone given its slant height
A cone has radius $5\ \text{cm}$ and slant height $13\ \text{cm}$. Find its volume.
@step The volume formula needs the **height**: $h = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 12\ \text{cm}$
@step $V = \tfrac13\pi(5)^2(12) = 100\pi$
@answer $V \approx 314.2\ \text{cm}^3$
:::

:::mistake Using slant height in a volume formula
Volume formulas always use the perpendicular **height**. If you are given the slant height, use the Pythagorean theorem first.
:::

:::summary
- Prism or cylinder: $V = Bh$; cylinder $V = \pi r^2 h$.
- Pyramid or cone: $V = \tfrac13 Bh$; cone $V = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h$.
- A pyramid (cone) holds one third of the prism (cylinder) with the same base and height.
- Volume uses the height, not the slant height.
- $1\ \text{cm}^3 = 1\ \text{mL}$ and $1000\ \text{cm}^3 = 1\ \text{L}$.
:::
