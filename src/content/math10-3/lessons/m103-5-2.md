Real floors, yards and walls are rarely perfect rectangles. A basement can be L-shaped, a wall has a window cut into it, and a slough in a pasture has no straight edges at all. In this lesson you will break awkward shapes into pieces you already know how to measure.

## Adding the pieces

:::definition Composite shape
A **composite shape** is made of two or more common shapes (rectangles, triangles, circles and so on) joined together.
:::

To find the area of a composite shape, split it into simple pieces that do not overlap, find each area, then add.

:::example An L-shaped kitchen floor
The L-shaped floor above is $8\ \text{m}$ along the bottom and $6\ \text{m}$ up the left side. The top edge is $4\ \text{m}$ and the short right edge is $3\ \text{m}$. Find the floor area.
@step Split the floor with the dashed line. The left rectangle is $4\ \text{m}$ by $6\ \text{m}$: $4 \times 6 = 24\ \text{m}^2$.
@step The right rectangle is $8 - 4 = 4\ \text{m}$ wide and $3\ \text{m}$ tall: $4 \times 3 = 12\ \text{m}^2$.
@step Add: $24 + 12 = 36$
@answer The kitchen floor has an area of $36\ \text{m}^2$.
:::

:::tip Find the missing sides
Plans often leave out some lengths. In an L-shape, a missing side is the difference of two sides you know: here, $8 - 4 = 4\ \text{m}$.
:::

## Subtracting the missing part

Sometimes it is easier to start with a big shape and **take away** the part that is not included: a window in a wall, a sink in a countertop, or the notch in an L-shape. For the floor above, $8 \times 6 - 4 \times 3 = 48 - 12 = 36\ \text{m}^2$, the same answer.

:::example Painting a wall
A wall is $14\ \text{ft}$ long and $8\ \text{ft}$ high. It has a window that is $4\ \text{ft}$ by $3\ \text{ft}$. What area needs paint?
@step Whole wall: $14 \times 8 = 112\ \text{ft}^2$
@step Window: $4 \times 3 = 12\ \text{ft}^2$
@answer Paint area: $112 - 12 = 100\ \text{ft}^2$
:::

:::example Plywood with a round hole
A $4\ \text{ft}$ by $8\ \text{ft}$ sheet of plywood has a circular hole with a diameter of $2\ \text{ft}$ cut out of it for a pipe. How much plywood is left, to the nearest tenth of a square foot?
@step Rectangle: $4 \times 8 = 32\ \text{ft}^2$
@step Hole: $r = 2 \div 2 = 1\ \text{ft}$, so $A = \pi(1)^2 \approx 3.14\ \text{ft}^2$
@step Subtract: $32 - 3.14 = 28.86$
@answer About $28.9\ \text{ft}^2$ of plywood is left.
:::

:::mistake Counting a piece twice
When you split a shape, the pieces must not overlap. If two rectangles share a corner region, that region gets counted twice and your area is too big. Draw your split line on the sketch before you calculate.
:::

A window with a semicircular top works the same way: rectangle **plus** half a circle. A window $36\ \text{in}$ wide with a rectangular part $48\ \text{in}$ tall has area $36 \times 48 + \pi(18)^2 \div 2 \approx 1728 + 508.94 \approx 2236.9\ \text{in}^2$.

## Estimating irregular shapes with a grid

:::definition Irregular shape
An **irregular shape** has curved or uneven edges that do not match any formula, like a pond, an oil spill or a patch of hail damage.
:::

To estimate its area, draw it on a grid:

1. Count the squares that are **completely** inside the shape.
2. Count the squares that are **partly** inside.
3. Partly covered squares are about half full on average, so add **half** of them.

:::

:::example A slough in a pasture
A rancher sketches a slough on a grid where each square is $1\ \text{m}^2$, as shown. Estimate its area.
@step Full squares: $11$
@step Partly covered squares: $14$, worth about $14 \div 2 = 7$ full squares.
@step Estimate: $11 + 7 = 18$
@answer The slough covers about $18\ \text{m}^2$.
:::

:::tip Estimates can differ a little
Two people may count one or two squares differently. That is fine: an estimate is supposed to be close, not exact. If each square stands for more than $1$ square unit (say $5\ \text{m}$ by $5\ \text{m} = 25\ \text{m}^2$), multiply your count by the area of one square.
:::

## Splitting into rectangles and triangles

When an irregular shape has straight edges, you can find its area exactly by splitting it into rectangles and right triangles.

:::figure
{"type":"plane","grid":{"xMin":0,"xMax":80,"yMin":0,"yMax":70,"step":10},"polylines":[{"points":[[10,10],[70,10],[70,40],[10,60],[10,10]],"color":"brand"}],"segments":[{"from":[10,40],"to":[70,40],"dashed":true,"color":"muted"}],"caption":"plan of a building lot on a grid of 10 m squares"}
:::

:::example A building lot
The plan of a building lot is drawn on a grid where each square is $10\ \text{m}$ by $10\ \text{m}$. Find its area.
@step The bottom edge is 6 squares $= 60\ \text{m}$. The right edge is 3 squares $= 30\ \text{m}$. The left edge is 5 squares $= 50\ \text{m}$.
@step Rectangle below the dashed line: $60 \times 30 = 1800\ \text{m}^2$
@step Triangle on top: legs $60\ \text{m}$ and $50 - 30 = 20\ \text{m}$, so $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 60 \times 20 = 600\ \text{m}^2$
@answer Total area: $1800 + 600 = 2400\ \text{m}^2$
:::

:::check m103-area-irregular 3
:::

:::summary
- A composite shape is made of common shapes. Split it into pieces that do not overlap and add their areas.
- Or start with a big shape and subtract the parts that are missing (windows, holes, notches).
- Missing side lengths are often the difference of two given sides.
- Estimate an irregular area on a grid: full squares + half of the partly covered squares.
- If each grid square is bigger than one square unit, multiply by the area of one square.
:::
