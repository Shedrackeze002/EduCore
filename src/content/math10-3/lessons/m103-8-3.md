Carpenters, surveyors, roofers and safety officers solve right triangles every day, but nobody hands them a labelled diagram. The hard part is turning the words of a problem into a sketch. After that, SOH CAH TOA does the rest.

## A plan for any problem

Use the same five steps every time:

1. **Sketch** the situation and mark the right angle.
2. **Mark** the angle you know (or want) and the sides you know (or want).
3. **Name** the sides relative to that angle: opposite, adjacent, hypotenuse.
4. **Choose** the ratio with SOH CAH TOA, write the equation and solve it.
5. **Check**: Is the hypotenuse the longest side? Is the size sensible? Did you include units?

:::example How high is the kite?
A kite string is $60$ m long and makes an angle of $40^\circ$ with the ground. How high is the kite? Ignore the sag in the string and the height of your hands, and round to the nearest tenth of a metre.
@step Sketch: the string is the hypotenuse. The height $h$ is opposite the $40^\circ$ angle.
@step Opposite and hypotenuse: SOH. $\sin 40^\circ = \dfrac{h}{60}$
@step $h = 60\sin 40^\circ \approx 60 \times 0.6428$
@answer $h \approx 38.6\ \text{m}$. Check: the height is shorter than the $60$ m string, as it must be.
:::

## Angles of elevation and depression

Many problems describe an angle by where you are looking.

:::definition Angle of elevation and angle of depression
- The **angle of elevation** is measured **up** from the horizontal to your line of sight.
- The **angle of depression** is measured **down** from the horizontal to your line of sight.
:::

When you look down from the top of a building, the horizontal line at your eyes is parallel to the ground. So the angle of depression from the top is **equal** to the angle of elevation from the ground (they are alternate angles). Put that angle at the bottom of your triangle.

:::example From the top of a grain elevator
From the top of a $32$ m grain elevator, a worker sees a truck at an angle of depression of $24^\circ$. How far is the truck from the base of the elevator? Round to the nearest tenth of a metre.
@step The angle at the truck is also $24^\circ$ (alternate angles).
@step Relative to that angle, the height ($32$ m) is opposite and the distance $d$ is adjacent. Use TOA: $\tan 24^\circ = \dfrac{32}{d}$
@step The unknown is on the bottom, so divide: $d = \dfrac{32}{\tan 24^\circ} \approx \dfrac{32}{0.4452}$
@answer $d \approx 71.9\ \text{m}$
:::

:::mistake Measuring from the wrong line
An angle of depression is measured from the **horizontal**, not from the wall of the building. If you put the $24^\circ$ between the line of sight and the wall, you get the wrong triangle and the wrong answer.
:::

## Ramps and roofs

On the job, a slope is often given as a ratio of rise to run instead of an angle. To change a slope ratio into an angle, use $\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}\right)$.

:::definition Roof pitch
**Pitch** describes how steep a roof is, as the rise for every $12$ units of horizontal run. A **6/12 pitch** rises $6$ in for every $12$ in of run.
:::

- A wheelchair ramp with a 1 : 12 slope makes an angle of $\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{12}\right) \approx 4.8^\circ$ with the ground.
- A roof with a 6/12 pitch makes an angle of $\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{6}{12}\right) \approx 26.6^\circ$ with the horizontal.

:::example A rafter for a 6/12 roof
Each rafter on a 6/12 roof covers a horizontal run of $12$ ft. Find the angle of the roof and the length of each rafter, to the nearest tenth.
@step Angle: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{6}{12}$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.5) \approx 26.6^\circ$.
@step The run is adjacent and the rafter $L$ is the hypotenuse: $\cos\theta = \dfrac{12}{L}$, so $L = \dfrac{12}{\cos\theta}$.
@step Use the full angle from your calculator: $L \approx 13.4$ ft.
@answer $\theta \approx 26.6^\circ$ and $L \approx 13.4\ \text{ft}$. Check: the roof rises $6$ ft over the $12$ ft run, and $\sqrt{12^2 + 6^2} \approx 13.4$.
:::

:::example Planning a wheelchair ramp
Suppose the building code allows a ramp angle of at most $4.8^\circ$. A ramp must rise $0.6$ m to a doorway. What is the shortest horizontal run allowed? Round to the nearest tenth of a metre.
@step The rise is opposite the angle and the run $d$ is adjacent: $\tan 4.8^\circ = \dfrac{0.6}{d}$
@step $d = \dfrac{0.6}{\tan 4.8^\circ}$
@answer $d \approx 7.1\ \text{m}$, close to the $12 \times 0.6 = 7.2$ m you get from the 1 : 12 rule.
:::

## Ladders and guy wires

A common safety guideline says a ladder should make an angle of about $75^\circ$ with the ground. Workers often use the **4-to-1 rule**: move the foot $1$ m out from the wall for every $4$ m of height. That gives an angle of $\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{4}{1}\right) \approx 76^\circ$.

:::example Setting up a ladder
An $8$ m ladder is set at $75^\circ$ to the ground. How far from the wall is its foot, and how high up the wall does it reach? Round to the nearest tenth of a metre.
@step The ladder is the hypotenuse. The foot distance $d$ is adjacent: $d = 8\cos 75^\circ \approx 2.1$ m.
@step The height $h$ is opposite: $h = 8\sin 75^\circ \approx 7.7$ m.
@answer The foot is about $2.1\ \text{m}$ from the wall and the ladder reaches about $7.7\ \text{m}$ up the wall.
:::

:::tip Guy wires
A guy wire, the tower and the ground make a right triangle. The wire is the hypotenuse. The height where the wire is attached is opposite the angle at the ground, and the distance from the tower to the anchor is adjacent.
:::

## Two-step problems

Some problems need more than one step. You might find a length and then add to it, or use two triangles and subtract.

:::example A transit on a tripod
A surveyor's transit sits on a tripod $1.5$ m above the ground, $40$ m from an office building. The angle of elevation from the transit to the top of the building is $35^\circ$. How tall is the building? Round to the nearest tenth of a metre.
@step The triangle starts at the transit, not at the ground. The $40$ m is adjacent and $x$, the height above the transit, is opposite. Use TOA: $x = 40\tan 35^\circ \approx 28.01$ m.
@step Add the height of the transit: $28.01 + 1.5 = 29.51$ m.
@answer The building is about $29.5\ \text{m}$ tall.
:::

:::mistake Rounding too early
Keep all the digits on your calculator until the last step. Rounding an angle or a length in the middle of a problem can change your final answer.
:::

:::summary
- Sketch, label, name the sides, choose the ratio, solve, then check.
- Angles of elevation and depression are measured from the horizontal. The angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the bottom.
- A slope ratio becomes an angle with $\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}\right)$: a 1 : 12 ramp is about $4.8^\circ$ and a 6/12 roof is about $26.6^\circ$.
- A ladder should make about $75^\circ$ with the ground.
- In two-step problems, keep every digit until the end.
:::
