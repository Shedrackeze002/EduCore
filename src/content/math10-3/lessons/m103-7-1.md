A carpenter squaring a deck frame, a roofer cutting a rafter and an electrician running conduit across a ceiling all use the same rule about right triangles. It is more than 2500 years old, and it still saves time and material on job sites every day.

## Right triangles and the hypotenuse

A **right triangle** has one $90^\circ$ angle, shown by a small square in the corner. The two sides that meet at the right angle are called the **legs**. The side across from the right angle is the **hypotenuse**.

:::definition Hypotenuse and legs
The **hypotenuse** is the side across from the right angle. It is always the **longest** side of a right triangle. The other two sides are the **legs**.
:::

:::mistake The hypotenuse is not always at the bottom
A triangle can be turned or flipped any way. Do not look for the "slanted" side. Find the right-angle box, then look straight across from it. That side is the hypotenuse.
:::

## The Pythagorean theorem

Draw a square on each side of a right triangle. The areas of the two squares on the legs add up to the area of the square on the hypotenuse. For a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5: $3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5^2$.

:::definition Pythagorean theorem
In a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$,
$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$
:::

:::example Finding the hypotenuse
A right triangle has legs of $6\ \text{cm}$ and $8\ \text{cm}$. How long is the hypotenuse?
@step $c^2 = 6^2 + 8^2$
@step $c^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$
@step $c = \sqrt{100} = 10$
@answer The hypotenuse is $10\ \text{cm}$ long.
:::

Most answers are not whole numbers. Use the $\sqrt{\ }$ key on your calculator and round at the **end**.

:::example Rounding the answer
A right triangle has legs of $5\ \text{m}$ and $9\ \text{m}$. Find the hypotenuse to the nearest tenth of a metre.
@step $c^2 = 5^2 + 9^2 = 25 + 81 = 106$
@step $c = \sqrt{106} \approx 10.2956...$
@answer The hypotenuse is about $10.3\ \text{m}$ long.
:::

:::tip Does the answer make sense?
The hypotenuse must be longer than either leg, but shorter than the two legs added together. Here, $10.3$ is more than $9$ and less than $5 + 9 = 14$.
:::

## Finding a leg

Sometimes you know the hypotenuse and one leg. Rearrange the formula so the unknown leg is by itself: subtract the known leg's square from both sides.

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b^2 = c^2 - a^2$$

:::example A whole-number leg
The hypotenuse of a right triangle is $13\ \text{ft}$ and one leg is $5\ \text{ft}$. Find the other leg, $x$.
@step $5^2 + x^2 = 13^2$
@step Rearrange: $x^2 = 13^2 - 5^2 = 169 - 25 = 144$
@step $x = \sqrt{144} = 12$
@answer The other leg is $12\ \text{ft}$ long.
:::

:::example A leg with rounding
The hypotenuse is $15\ \text{cm}$ and one leg is $9.5\ \text{cm}$. Find the other leg to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.
@step $x^2 = 15^2 - 9.5^2 = 225 - 90.25 = 134.75$
@step $x = \sqrt{134.75} \approx 11.608...$
@answer The other leg is about $11.6\ \text{cm}$ long.
:::

:::mistake Adding when you should subtract
If you add the squares when finding a **leg**, your answer will be longer than the hypotenuse. That is impossible. A leg is always shorter than the hypotenuse, so check your answer against it.
:::

## Is it a right triangle? The 3-4-5 method

The theorem also works in reverse. If the squares of the two shorter sides add up **exactly** to the square of the longest side, the triangle has a right angle. If not, it does not.

:::example Testing three sides
A triangle has sides of $7\ \text{m}$, $9\ \text{m}$ and $11\ \text{m}$. Is it a right triangle?
@step The longest side is $11$, so test $7^2 + 9^2 = 49 + 81 = 130$.
@step $11^2 = 121$
@answer $130 \ne 121$, so it is **not** a right triangle.
:::

Builders use this to check that a corner is **square** (exactly $90^\circ$). Measure $3$ units along one side and $4$ units along the other. If the diagonal between the marks is exactly $5$ units, the corner is square. Bigger multiples are more accurate: $6$-$8$-$10$ ft or $9$-$12$-$15$ ft.

:::example Squaring a deck frame
A framer measures $6\ \text{ft}$ along one side of a deck frame and $8\ \text{ft}$ along the other. The diagonal measures $9\ \text{ft}\ 10\ \text{in}$. Is the corner square?
@step For a square corner, $6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$, so the diagonal should be $\sqrt{100} = 10\ \text{ft}$.
@step $9\ \text{ft}\ 10\ \text{in}$ is $2\ \text{in}$ shorter than $10\ \text{ft}$.
@step A short diagonal means the two sides are pulled too close together.
@answer The corner is not square. The angle is **less** than $90^\circ$, so the framer pushes the sides apart until the diagonal reads $10\ \text{ft}$.
:::

:::mistake Adding instead of multiplying
To get bigger measurements, **multiply** 3, 4 and 5 by the same number. Adding the same amount does not work: $4$-$5$-$6$ is not a right triangle, because $4^2 + 5^2 = 41$ but $6^2 = 36$.
:::

:::summary
- The hypotenuse is across from the right angle and is always the longest side.
- Pythagorean theorem: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
- Hypotenuse: square the legs, **add**, take the square root.
- Leg: rearrange to $b^2 = c^2 - a^2$, **subtract**, take the square root.
- If $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ exactly, the triangle has a right angle. The 3-4-5 method (or 6-8-10, 9-12-15) checks a corner for square.
- Round only at the end, and check that your answer makes sense.
:::
