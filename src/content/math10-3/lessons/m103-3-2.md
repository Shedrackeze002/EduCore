Canada uses the SI system, but walk into any lumber yard and you will buy 8-foot 2-by-4s. Tires are sized in inches, hay is sold by the ton, and recipes from the United States use cups and gallons. Workers in the trades need to be comfortable with **imperial** units too.

## Imperial units of length

Imperial units are not based on powers of 10, so you need to know the relationships.

:::definition Imperial length units
$$12\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{ft}, \qquad 3\ \text{ft} = 1\ \text{yd}, \qquad 36\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{yd}, \qquad 5280\ \text{ft} = 1\ \text{mi} \ (= 1760\ \text{yd})$$
An **inch** is about the width of your thumb, a **foot** is about the length of an adult's shoe, a **yard** is about one big step, and a **mile** is about a 20-minute walk.
:::

The same rule as in SI applies: to a **smaller** unit, **multiply**; to a **larger** unit, **divide**.

:::example Mixed units to inches
A workbench in a shop is $5\ \text{ft}\ 7\ \text{in}$ long. What is this length in inches?
@step Change the feet to inches: $5 \times 12 = 60\ \text{in}$.
@step Add the extra inches: $60 + 7 = 67$.
@answer $5\ \text{ft}\ 7\ \text{in} = 67\ \text{in}$
:::

:::example Inches to feet and inches
A board is $89\ \text{in}$ long. Write this in feet and inches.
@step $89 \div 12 = 7$ remainder $5$, because $7 \times 12 = 84$ and $89 - 84 = 5$.
@step The quotient is the feet and the remainder is the inches.
@answer $89\ \text{in} = 7\ \text{ft}\ 5\ \text{in}$
:::

:::mistake A decimal is not feet and inches
A calculator shows $89 \div 12 = 7.4166\ldots$, but that does **not** mean 7 ft 4 in. The decimal is a fraction of a foot: $0.4166\ldots \times 12 = 5$ inches. Use the remainder instead.
:::

## Mass and capacity in imperial units

In everyday speech we say "weight", but these units measure mass.

:::definition Imperial mass units
$$16\ \text{oz} = 1\ \text{lb}, \qquad 2000\ \text{lb} = 1\ \text{ton}$$
The ton used in North America is the **short ton** of 2000 pounds.
:::

:::example Pounds and ounces
A turkey weighs $7\ \text{lb}\ 5\ \text{oz}$. What is its mass in ounces?
@step $7\ \text{lb} = 7 \times 16 = 112\ \text{oz}$
@step $112 + 5 = 117$
@answer The turkey weighs $117\ \text{oz}$.
:::

For capacity, recipes and products from the United States use **US** units:

| US unit | Equal to |
|---|---|
| 1 gallon (gal) | 4 quarts (qt) |
| 1 quart | 2 pints (pt) |
| 1 pint | 2 cups |
| 1 cup | 8 fluid ounces (fl oz) |

So $1\ \text{gal} = 16$ cups $= 128\ \text{fl oz}$.

:::example Gallons to cups
A cafeteria buys $3\ \text{gal}$ of milk (US). How many cups is that?
@step $1\ \text{gal} = 4\ \text{qt} = 8\ \text{pt} = 16$ cups.
@step $3 \times 16 = 48$
@answer $3$ US gallons $= 48$ cups
:::

:::tip Build the chain
If you forget how many cups are in a gallon, multiply along the chain: $4 \times 2 \times 2 = 16$.
:::

## US gallons and imperial gallons

Here is a trap: the United States and Britain both use the word **gallon**, but they are **different sizes**. Older Canadian recipes, British cookbooks and equipment made in the United Kingdom use the **imperial** (British) gallon.

:::definition US and imperial gallons
- $1$ US gallon $\approx 3.785\ \text{L}$, and it holds $128$ US fluid ounces.
- $1$ imperial gallon $\approx 4.546\ \text{L}$, and it holds $160$ imperial fluid ounces.

Both gallons split into $4$ quarts or $8$ pints, so the imperial quart and pint are also bigger than the US ones. An imperial pint is $20$ imperial fl oz.
:::

:::example Comparing fuel tanks
A British-made boat has a $12$-gallon (imperial) fuel tank. How many litres is that, and how many US gallons?
@step Litres: $12 \times 4.546 = 54.552\ \text{L}$
@step US gallons: $54.552 \div 3.785 \approx 14.41$
@answer About $54.6\ \text{L}$, or about $14.4$ US gallons
:::

:::mistake Assuming a gallon is a gallon
If you fill that boat's tank with "12 gallons" from a US-gallon pump, you will be about $2.4$ US gallons short. Always check **which** gallon a recipe, manual or label uses.
:::

:::note The fluid ounce surprise
The US fluid ounce ($\approx 29.57\ \text{mL}$) is a little **larger** than the imperial fluid ounce ($\approx 28.41\ \text{mL}$). The imperial gallon is still bigger because it holds 160 of them instead of 128.
:::

## Multi-step problems

Real jobs often combine several of these steps.

:::example Adding trim
A carpenter needs two pieces of trim: $4\ \text{ft}\ 9\ \text{in}$ and $3\ \text{ft}\ 7\ \text{in}$. What is the total length?
@step Add feet and inches separately: $4 + 3 = 7\ \text{ft}$ and $9 + 7 = 16\ \text{in}$.
@step $16\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{ft}\ 4\ \text{in}$, so carry $1$ foot.
@answer $8\ \text{ft}\ 4\ \text{in}$
:::

:::summary
- Length: $12\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{ft}$, $3\ \text{ft} = 1\ \text{yd}$, $5280\ \text{ft} = 1\ \text{mi}$.
- Mass: $16\ \text{oz} = 1\ \text{lb}$, $2000\ \text{lb} = 1$ (short) ton.
- US capacity: $1\ \text{gal} = 4\ \text{qt} = 8\ \text{pt} = 16$ cups $= 128\ \text{fl oz}$.
- An imperial gallon ($\approx 4.546\ \text{L}$) is bigger than a US gallon ($\approx 3.785\ \text{L}$). Always check which one is meant.
- For feet and inches, divide by 12 and use the remainder, not the decimal.
:::
