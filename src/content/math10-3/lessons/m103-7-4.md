Blueprints, site plans and road maps are all drawings of something much bigger, made to scale. Similar figures let a builder read a real length off a plan, and even let a farmer measure a grain elevator with nothing but a shadow and a tape measure.

## Finding a missing side

When two figures are similar, every side is multiplied by the same scale factor. Find the scale factor from one pair of matching sides you know, then use it on the side you want.

:::example Two similar rectangles
Rectangle A is $5\ \text{m}$ long and $3\ \text{m}$ wide. Rectangle B is similar to A and is $12.5\ \text{m}$ long. How wide is rectangle B?
@step Scale factor from the lengths: $k = \dfrac{12.5}{5} = 2.5$
@step Width of B: $3 \times 2.5 = 7.5$
@answer Rectangle B is $7.5\ \text{m}$ wide.
:::

When polygons are named, the **order of the letters** tells you which sides match. In $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$ (read "is similar to"), $A$ matches $D$, $B$ matches $E$ and $C$ matches $F$. So $AB$ matches $DE$, $BC$ matches $EF$ and $CA$ matches $FD$.

:::example Using a proportion
$\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$, with $AB = 8\ \text{cm}$, $BC = 6\ \text{cm}$ and $DE = 12\ \text{cm}$. Find $EF$.
@step $AB$ matches $DE$, and $BC$ matches $EF$. Let $x = EF$.
@step Set up a proportion: $\dfrac{x}{6} = \dfrac{12}{8}$
@step Multiply both sides by 6: $x = 6 \times \dfrac{12}{8} = 9$
@answer $EF = 9\ \text{cm}$.
:::

:::mistake Matching the wrong sides
Do not match sides by where they happen to sit on the page. One triangle may be flipped or turned. Use the letter order, or match longest with longest and shortest with shortest.
:::

## The shadow method

At the same moment, the sun's rays hit every object at the same angle. So an object, its shadow and the sun's ray make a right triangle that is **similar** to the triangle made by anything else nearby. Measure something short, and you can work out the height of something tall.

:::

:::example How tall is the grain elevator?
Late in the afternoon, Aiyana is $1.7\ \text{m}$ tall and casts a shadow $2.5\ \text{m}$ long. At the same time, a grain elevator casts a shadow $30\ \text{m}$ long. How tall is the grain elevator?
@step The two triangles are similar, so $\dfrac{\text{height}}{\text{shadow}}$ is the same for both.
@step $\dfrac{h}{30} = \dfrac{1.7}{2.5}$
@step $h = 30 \times \dfrac{1.7}{2.5} = 20.4$
@answer The grain elevator is $20.4\ \text{m}$ tall.
:::

:::tip Triangles inside triangles
A vertical post under a ramp makes a small triangle inside the big one. They share the angle at the bottom, so they are similar. If a $0.5\ \text{m}$ post stands $2\ \text{m}$ from the bottom and the ramp's run is $6\ \text{m}$, the top is $6 \times \dfrac{0.5}{2} = 1.5\ \text{m}$ high.
:::

## Reading a scale

:::definition Scale and scale diagram
A **scale diagram** is a drawing that is similar to a real object. The **scale** compares a length on the drawing with the real length. It can be written in words ($1\ \text{cm}$ represents $2\ \text{m}$), as a ratio ($1:50$), or with units ($\tfrac{1}{4}\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{ft}$).
:::

A scale of $1:50$ means every real length is $50$ times the length on the drawing, in the **same** unit.

:::example A metric floor plan
A floor plan has a scale of $1:50$. A wall measures $9.2\ \text{cm}$ on the plan. How long is the real wall, in metres?
@step Real length: $9.2 \times 50 = 460\ \text{cm}$
@step Change to metres: $460 \div 100 = 4.6$
@answer The wall is $4.6\ \text{m}$ long.
:::

:::example An imperial blueprint
A blueprint uses $\tfrac{1}{4}\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{ft}$. A room is $14\ \text{ft}$ long. How long is it on the blueprint? A hallway measures $3\tfrac{3}{4}\ \text{in}$ on the blueprint. How long is it in real life?
@step Each foot is drawn as $\tfrac{1}{4}\ \text{in}$, so $14\ \text{ft}$ is drawn as $14 \div 4 = 3\tfrac{1}{2}\ \text{in}$.
@step Each whole inch on the blueprint stands for $4\ \text{ft}$, so $3\tfrac{3}{4} \times 4 = 15$.
@answer The room is drawn $3\tfrac{1}{2}\ \text{in}$ long, and the hallway is $15\ \text{ft}$ long.
:::

:::mistake Forgetting to convert
With a ratio scale like $1:50$, multiplying gives the real length in the **same unit as the drawing** (usually centimetres). If the question asks for metres, divide by 100 at the end.
:::

## Maps and finding the scale

Maps use large ratios. On a $1:50\,000$ topographic map, $1\ \text{cm}$ stands for $50\,000\ \text{cm}$, which is $500\ \text{m}$.

:::example A hiking trail
On a $1:50\,000$ map, a trail measures $7.4\ \text{cm}$. How long is the real trail, in kilometres?
@step $7.4 \times 50\,000 = 370\,000\ \text{cm}$
@step $1\ \text{km} = 100\,000\ \text{cm}$, so $370\,000 \div 100\,000 = 3.7$
@answer The trail is $3.7\ \text{km}$ long.
:::

:::example Finding the scale
On a drawing, a $6\ \text{m}$ wall is drawn $12\ \text{cm}$ long. Write the scale as $1:n$.
@step Same units: $6\ \text{m} = 600\ \text{cm}$.
@step $n = \dfrac{600}{12} = 50$
@answer The scale is $1:50$.
:::

:::

:::summary
- In similar figures, find the scale factor from one pair of matching sides, then multiply (or set up a proportion).
- The letter order in $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$ tells you which sides match.
- Shadow method: $\dfrac{\text{height}}{\text{shadow}}$ is the same for objects measured at the same time.
- A scale such as $1:50$ means real length $= 50 \times$ drawn length, in the same unit. Convert units at the end.
- To find a scale, put both lengths in the same unit, then divide real by drawn.
:::
