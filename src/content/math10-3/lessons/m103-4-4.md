Before a job starts, someone has to work out how much material to buy and what it will cost. Order too little and the crew stands around waiting. Order too much and the profit ends up in the scrap bin.

## How many pieces can you cut?

To find how many pieces fit in a board, pipe or rod, **divide** the full length by the length of one piece. Only whole pieces are useful, so always round **down**.

:::definition Waste
**Waste** is the material left over that is too short to use. Good planning keeps waste small.
:::

:::example Cutting copper pipe
A plumber cuts pieces $45\ \text{cm}$ long from a $3\ \text{m}$ length of copper pipe. How many whole pieces can she cut, and how much is left over?
@step Use the same unit: $3\ \text{m} = 300\ \text{cm}$
@step $300 \div 45 \approx 6.67$, so round down to $6$ pieces.
@step Used: $6 \times 45 = 270\ \text{cm}$. Leftover: $300 - 270 = 30\ \text{cm}$
@answer $6$ whole pieces, with $30\ \text{cm}$ left over.
:::

:::mistake Rounding up the number of pieces
$6.67$ pieces does **not** mean you can cut $7$. The seventh piece would be only part of a piece. When counting pieces you can cut, round down.
:::

## Allowing for the saw kerf

Every saw cut turns a thin strip of material into sawdust. This strip is the **saw kerf**. For a table saw blade it is about $3\ \text{mm}$ or $\tfrac18$ in. When you cut many pieces, the kerf adds up, so each piece really uses its own length **plus** one kerf.

:::example Shelf pieces with a 3 mm kerf
Aiyana cuts shelf pieces $350\ \text{mm}$ long from a board $2.4\ \text{m}$ long. Each cut wastes $3\ \text{mm}$. How many pieces can she cut, and how long is the leftover?
@step Board: $2.4\ \text{m} = 2400\ \text{mm}$
@step Each piece uses $350 + 3 = 353\ \text{mm}$
@step $2400 \div 353 \approx 6.8$, so round down to $6$ pieces.
@step Used: $6 \times 353 = 2118\ \text{mm}$. Leftover: $2400 - 2118 = 282\ \text{mm}$
@answer $6$ pieces, with a leftover piece $282\ \text{mm}$ long.
:::

When you need many pieces, first find how many you get from **one** board. Then decide how many boards to buy. This time round **up**, because a store will not sell part of a board.

:::example How many boards to buy
Omar needs $32$ pieces, each $70\ \text{cm}$ long, and boards come in $2.4\ \text{m}$ lengths. Pieces cannot be joined. How many boards must he buy?
@step One board: $240 \div 70 \approx 3.43$, so $3$ pieces per board.
@step Boards: $32 \div 3 \approx 10.67$. Round up to $11$.
@answer Omar must buy $11$ boards.
:::

## Buying by the linear foot or metre

Trim, fencing, wire, pipe and rope are priced by length.

:::definition Linear foot
A **linear foot** is simply one foot of length, measured along the material. "Baseboard costs \$2.19 per linear foot" means each foot of baseboard costs \$2.19, whatever its width.
:::

To find the cost, multiply: **cost = length × price per unit of length**. Round money to the nearest cent.

:::example Baseboard for a room
A room is $14\ \text{ft}$ by $11\ \text{ft}$, with one $3\ \text{ft}$ doorway that gets no baseboard. Baseboard costs \$2.49 per linear foot. What does the baseboard cost?
@step Perimeter: $2(14 + 11) = 50\ \text{ft}$
@step Subtract the doorway: $50 - 3 = 47\ \text{ft}$
@step Cost: $47 \times 2.49 = 117.03$
@answer The baseboard costs \$117.03.
:::

:::tip Check the units
If the price is per foot but the length is in inches or yards, convert first: $12\ \text{in} = 1\ \text{ft}$ and $1\ \text{yd} = 3\ \text{ft}$.
:::

## Buying whole lengths and adding GST

Building supply stores often sell trim in fixed lengths, such as $8$, $12$ or $16$ ft. Then you must buy whole lengths, and the store adds tax. In these questions, add $5\%$ GST when the question tells you to.

:::example Whole lengths plus GST
The room above needs $47\ \text{ft}$ of baseboard. Baseboard is sold in $12\ \text{ft}$ lengths at \$24.50 each, and pieces can be joined along a wall. GST of 5% is added. What is the total cost?
@step Lengths: $47 \div 12 \approx 3.92$. Round up to $4$.
@step Subtotal: $4 \times 24.50 = 98.00$
@step GST: $0.05 \times 98.00 = 4.90$
@step Total: $98.00 + 4.90 = 102.90$
@answer Buy $4$ lengths for a total of \$102.90.
:::

:::mistake Paying for 3.92 lengths
The store sells whole lengths only. $3.92$ lengths means you need $4$. Multiply the price by the **whole** number you buy, then add the tax.
:::

:::check m103-linear-cost 3
:::

:::summary
- Pieces you can cut: divide and round **down**. Leftover = full length − pieces × piece length.
- Saw kerf: each piece uses its own length plus one cut.
- Boards or lengths to buy: divide and round **up**.
- Cost = length × price per linear foot (or metre), rounded to the nearest cent.
- With fixed lengths, round up to whole lengths, find the subtotal, then add GST (total = subtotal × 1.05 for 5%).
:::
