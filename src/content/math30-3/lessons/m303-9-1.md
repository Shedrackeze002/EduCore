Puzzles and games are good practice for the kind of thinking every trade needs: start from what you know for sure, rule things out, and plan a few moves ahead. Each puzzle in this lesson has exactly one answer, and each game has a strategy you can learn.

## Number puzzles: start from what you know

:::definition Strategy
A **strategy** is a plan that works every time, not just a lucky guess. Good puzzle strategies: start with the step that has only one possibility, work backward from the goal, and use a variable when nothing else works.
:::

In a **number pyramid**, each brick is the sum of the two bricks directly below it. Look for three bricks in a triangle where you know two of them: add if you know the bottom two, subtract if you know the top one.

:::example A pyramid that needs algebra
Find the missing number in the bottom row.
@step No triangle has two known bricks, so let the missing number be $x$.
@step Middle row: $6 + x$ and $x + 9$.
@step Top: $(6 + x) + (x + 9) = 2x + 15$.
@step $2x + 15 = 37$, so $2x = 22$ and $x = 11$.
@step Check: $6 + 11 = 17$, $11 + 9 = 20$, and $17 + 20 = 37$. ✓
@answer The missing number is $11$.
:::

## Grid puzzles: Sudoku and KenKen

In a $4 \times 4$ **Sudoku**, each row, column and $2 \times 2$ box holds $1$, $2$, $3$ and $4$ once. Two rules solve most puzzles:

- **Only one number fits this cell:** its row, column and box already hold the other three numbers.
- **Only one cell fits this number:** in a row, column or box, a missing number can go in only one empty cell.

:::example Two Sudoku moves
(a) Find the cell marked $?$. (b) In row 4, where does the $2$ go?
@step (a) Row 3 already has $2$ and $1$, and column 3 has $3$. The only number left is $4$.
@step (b) Row 4 is missing $2$ and $4$, in columns 1 and 3. Column 1 already has a $2$ (in row 3).
@step So the $2$ in row 4 cannot go in column 1. It goes in column 3.
@answer (a) $? = 4$. (b) The $2$ goes in row 4, column 3.

In **KenKen**, each row and column holds each number once, and each cage (outlined in solid lines) must give the number in its corner with the operation shown. Start with cages that can only be made one way: in a $4 \times 4$ grid, a two-cell "$7+$" cage must be $3$ and $4$, and a "$3-$" cage must be $4$ and $1$.

:::example A small KenKen
Use the numbers $1$, $2$ and $3$. Find the cell marked $?$.
@step Row 3 must hold $1$, $2$ and $3$.
@step The "$4+$" cage in row 3 has two cells, so it must hold $1$ and $3$ ($1 + 3 = 4$).
@step The only number left for row 3 is $2$, and it goes in the cell marked $?$.
@step Check the "$1-$" cage: the cell above $?$ is in row 2, which already has its $1$ in the one-cell cage, so it holds $3$, and $3 - 2 = 1$. ✓
@answer $? = 2$.
:::

## Logic grids

A **logic grid** matches people with things. Put ✗ where a clue rules a match out and ✓ where a match is certain. Each row and each column ends with exactly one ✓.

:::figure

:::

:::example Who is the welder?
Nadia, Owen and Tariq each have a different trade: electrician, plumber or welder. Clue 1: Owen is not the welder. Clue 2: The plumber is neither Nadia nor Owen.
@step Clue 2: cross out plumber for Nadia and Owen. Only Tariq is left for plumber, so Tariq is the plumber.
@step Owen is not the welder (clue 1) and not the plumber, so Owen is the electrician.
@step Nadia is left with welder.
@answer Nadia is the welder.
:::

:::mistake Guessing too early
Do not put a ✓ until the clues force it. A guess that looks right can break a clue you have not used yet. Use every clue, then check the final answer against all of them.
:::

## Weighing and crossing puzzles

**Weighing.** You have $12$ bolts and one is lighter. A balance scale has **three** outcomes (left lighter, right lighter, or balanced), so split into three groups: $4$, $4$ and $4$. Weigh two groups: the light bolt is in the lighter group, or in the group left off if they balance. Then weigh $1$ against $1$ from those $4$, leaving $2$ off; at worst $2$ remain, and one more weighing finds it. That is $3$ weighings. In general, $w$ weighings can handle up to $3^w$ items.

**Crossing a river.** Keep track of who is on each side after every trip, and remember that someone has to bring the boat back. When two light people and one heavy person must share a small boat, a useful pattern is: both light people cross, one comes back, the heavy person crosses, and the other light person comes back.

:::tip Count the trips back
After every crossing except the last, the boat must come back, so the number of crossings is always odd. Plan who will paddle back before you send anyone across.
:::

## Games: finding the winning move

**Take-away games.** Work backward from the end. With $1$ to $3$ counters taken per turn and the last counter winning, leaving your opponent $4$ means they lose: whatever they take, you take the rest. So leave a multiple of $4$. With $22$ counters, take $2$ to leave $20$.

When the allowed moves are unusual, list the positions. With moves of $1$, $3$ or $4$, a position is **losing** if every move leads to a winning position. Checking $0$, $1$, $2$, … gives losing positions $0$, $2$, $7$, $9$, $14$, $16$, …: always move to one of these.

**Tic-tac-toe.** Choose your move in this order: (1) win if you can, (2) block if your opponent can win next turn, (3) make a **fork**, two threats at once, so your opponent can only block one.

:::example Making a fork
It is X’s turn. Where should X play?
@step X cannot win this turn, and O has no two-in-a-row to block.
@step If X plays the centre, X has two threats: the middle column (top-middle, centre, bottom-middle) and the diagonal (top-left, centre, bottom-right).
@step O can block only one of them, so X wins on the next turn.
@answer X should play the centre: it makes a fork.
:::

:::summary
- Start with the step that has only one possibility; each one unlocks another.
- Use a variable when nothing can be found directly (number pyramids).
- Sudoku: only one number fits a cell, or only one cell fits a number. KenKen: start with cages that can only be made one way.
- Logic grids: cross out what each clue rules out; each row and column has one ✓.
- Weighing: split into three groups. Crossings: plan who brings the boat back.
- Games: work backward to find losing positions; in tic-tac-toe, win, block, then fork.
:::
