Real problems do not come labelled "sine law" or "cosine law". A forestry crew locating a fire, a surveyor measuring a lake or a festival crew tracking a hot-air balloon must first sketch the triangle, mark what they know, and then choose a tool.

## Choosing a method

Start every problem with a sketch. Mark the known sides and angles, and circle what you want. Then ask one question: **do I have a matching pair** (a known side and the known angle opposite it)?

:::figure

:::

:::example Which law?
In $\triangle XYZ$, you know $x = 12$, $z = 15$ and $\angle Y = 64^\circ$. You want $y$. Which method should you use?
@step $\angle Y$ is between sides $x$ and $z$, so it is the contained angle.
@step Side $y$ is opposite $\angle Y$, but $y$ is unknown, so there is no complete matching pair.
@answer Two sides and the contained angle: use the cosine law, $y^2 = x^2 + z^2 - 2xz\cos Y$.
:::

:::mistake Using the sine law without a matching pair
"Two sides and an angle are known" is not enough for the sine law. The known angle must be opposite one of the known sides. If it is between them, use the cosine law.
:::

## Planning a solution with more than one step

Some problems need one law to set up the other. Plan the whole path before you calculate.

- **Two angles and the side between them:** find the third angle with the angle sum, then use the sine law.
- **Two sides and the contained angle, but you want an angle:** use the cosine law for the third side, then the sine law (or the cosine law) for the angle.
- **Two sides and an angle opposite one of them, but you want the third side:** use the sine law for a second angle, the angle sum for the third angle, then the sine law again.

:::note Information that does not fix a triangle
Three angles alone never fix a triangle: a small triangle and a large one can have the same angles. You always need at least one side.
:::

:::

:::example Measuring a lake
A surveyor at point $S$ measures $420\ \text{m}$ to point $P$ at one end of a lake and $360\ \text{m}$ to point $Q$ at the other end. The angle between the two lines of sight is $64^\circ$. How long is the lake, to the nearest metre?
@step The $64^\circ$ angle is between the two known sides (SAS): use the cosine law.
@step $PQ^2 = 420^2 + 360^2 - 2(420)(360)\cos 64^\circ \approx 176\,400 + 129\,600 - 132\,563.43 = 173\,436.57$
@step $PQ \approx \sqrt{173\,436.57} \approx 416.46$
@answer The lake is about $416\ \text{m}$ long.
:::

:::example Locating a fire
Two fire lookout towers, $A$ and $B$, are $18.5\ \text{km}$ apart. Both spot smoke at $F$. At $A$, the angle between the line to $B$ and the line to $F$ is $57^\circ$. At $B$, the angle between the line to $A$ and the line to $F$ is $68^\circ$. How far is the fire from tower $A$, to the nearest tenth of a kilometre?
@step The known side $AB$ is opposite $\angle F$. Angle sum: $\angle F = 180^\circ - 57^\circ - 68^\circ = 55^\circ$.
@step $AF$ is opposite $\angle B$: $\dfrac{AF}{\sin 68^\circ} = \dfrac{18.5}{\sin 55^\circ}$
@step $AF = \dfrac{18.5 \sin 68^\circ}{\sin 55^\circ} \approx 20.94$
@answer The fire is about $20.9\ \text{km}$ from tower $A$.
:::

## Problems with two steps

Heights and widths are usually measured straight up or straight across, which makes a right angle. Often you use the sine law or the cosine law to find a slanted side first, then SOH CAH TOA in a right triangle.

:::example How high is the balloon?
Two friends stand $640\ \text{m}$ apart on level ground, with a hot-air balloon between them. The angle of elevation of the balloon is $62^\circ$ from point $A$ and $55^\circ$ from point $B$. How high is the balloon, to the nearest metre?
@step Angle sum: $\angle H = 180^\circ - 62^\circ - 55^\circ = 63^\circ$, so $AB$ and $\angle H$ are a matching pair.
@step Sine law for $AH$, which is opposite $\angle B$: $AH = \dfrac{640 \sin 55^\circ}{\sin 63^\circ} \approx 588.39$
@step In the right triangle with hypotenuse $AH$, the height is opposite the $62^\circ$ angle: $h = AH \sin 62^\circ \approx 588.39 \times 0.8829 \approx 519.5$
@answer The balloon is about $520\ \text{m}$ above the ground.
:::

:::tip Keep the calculator values
Store the unrounded value of $AH$ in your calculator's memory and use it in the next step. Rounding in the middle can change your final answer.
:::

:::

:::summary
- Sketch the triangle, mark what you know, and look for a matching pair.
- Matching pair → sine law. Two angles and a side → angle sum, then sine law. SAS or SSS → cosine law. Right angle → SOH CAH TOA.
- Plan multi-step solutions before calculating; one law often sets up the other.
- Heights and widths need a right angle: find a slanted side first, then use a primary trigonometric ratio.
- Round only the final answer, and check that it is reasonable.
:::
