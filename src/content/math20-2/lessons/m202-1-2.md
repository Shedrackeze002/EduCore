No matter how many examples support a conjecture, a single example that goes against it is enough to show that it is false. Finding that one example is a powerful way to test an idea before you rely on it.

## What is a counterexample?

:::definition Counterexample
A **counterexample** is an example that shows a conjecture is false. One counterexample is enough to disprove a conjecture.
:::

:::example Prime numbers
Kiran conjectures: "All prime numbers are odd." Test the conjecture and find a counterexample if there is one.
@step Many primes are odd: $3$, $5$, $7$, $11$, $13$. These support the conjecture but do not prove it.
@step $2$ is prime (its only factors are 1 and 2), and $2$ is even.
@answer $2$ is a counterexample, so the conjecture is false.
:::

After a counterexample, you can often **revise** the conjecture so that it fits all the evidence: "All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd."

## A counterexample must meet the condition

Most conjectures have a **condition** (the group they talk about, or the "if" part) and a **conclusion**. A counterexample must meet the condition and still make the conclusion false.

:::example Divisible by 4 and by 8
Conjecture: "If a number is divisible by 4, then it is divisible by 8." Which of $6$, $12$ and $16$ is a counterexample?
@step $6$ is not divisible by 4, so it does not meet the condition. It cannot test the conjecture.
@step $16$ is divisible by 4 and by 8. It supports the conjecture.
@step $12 = 4 \times 3$ is divisible by 4, but $12 \div 8 = 1.5$ is not a whole number.
@answer $12$ is the counterexample.
:::

| Choice | Meets the condition? | Conclusion true? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| $6$ | No | — | does not test it |
| $16$ | Yes | Yes | supports it |
| $12$ | Yes | No | counterexample |

:::mistake Choosing an example that misses the condition
$6$ is not divisible by 8, but it is not divisible by 4 either, so it says nothing about the conjecture. Check the condition first, then the conclusion.
:::

## Conjectures that work for a long time

Some conjectures fit a huge number of cases and still fail. The expression $n^2 + n + 41$ gives a prime number for every whole number from $n = 0$ to $n = 39$:

$$0 + 0 + 41 = 41 \qquad 1 + 1 + 41 = 43 \qquad 4 + 2 + 41 = 47 \qquad \ldots \qquad 39^2 + 39 + 41 = 1601$$

But $40^2 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41 \times 41$, which is not prime. Forty supporting examples did not make the conjecture true.

:::example Regions in a circle
Points are placed on a circle and every pair of points is joined by a chord, with no three chords meeting at one point. With 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 points there are 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 regions. Malia conjectures that the number of regions doubles each time. Is she right?
@step The conjecture predicts $32$ regions for 6 points.
@step Drawing it carefully and counting gives only $31$ regions.
@answer $n = 6$ is a counterexample. The pattern 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 does not continue.
:::

:::tip Where to look for counterexamples
Try the cases that people forget: $0$ and $1$, negative numbers, fractions and decimals, very large numbers, and special shapes such as squares or right triangles.
:::

## Revising a conjecture

A counterexample does not always mean the idea is useless. It tells you where the conjecture needs a limit.

:::example Squares of numbers
Conjecture: "The square of a number is always greater than the number." Find a counterexample, then revise the conjecture.
@step Try a fraction: $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{1}{4}$, which is less than $\dfrac{1}{2}$. This is a counterexample.
@step Try $0$ and $1$: $0^2 = 0$ and $1^2 = 1$, so the square is not greater for these numbers either.
@step Numbers greater than 1 work: $3^2 = 9 > 3$. Negative numbers work too: $(-3)^2 = 9 > -3$.
@answer Revised conjecture: the square of a number is greater than the number when the number is greater than 1 or less than 0.
:::

:::summary
- A counterexample shows that a conjecture is false; one is enough.
- A counterexample must meet the condition of the conjecture and make its conclusion false.
- A conjecture can fit many cases and still be false, so supporting examples never prove it.
- Look for counterexamples among special cases such as 0, 1, negatives and fractions.
- Use a counterexample to revise a conjecture so it fits all the evidence.
:::
