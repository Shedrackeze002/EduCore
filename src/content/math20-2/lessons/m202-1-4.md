Puzzles and games are a great place to practise reasoning. As you play, you make conjectures about what works (inductive reasoning), and then you use logic to be certain (deductive reasoning).

## Number puzzles

:::definition Strategy
A **strategy** is a plan for solving a problem or winning a game. Useful puzzle strategies include making a list, using a chart, working backwards, solving a smaller version first, and looking for a pattern.
:::

For a number riddle, start with the clue that allows the fewest numbers. List them, then use each other clue to cross numbers off.

:::example A two-digit riddle
Ravi is thinking of a two-digit number. It is a multiple of 7. It is odd. The sum of its digits is 10. What is the number?
@step Two-digit multiples of 7: $14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, 63, 70, 77, 84, 91, 98$.
@step The odd ones: $21, 35, 49, 63, 77, 91$.
@step Digit sums: $3, 8, 13, 9, 14, 10$. Only $91$ has a digit sum of 10.
@answer The number is $91$.
:::

## Logic puzzles

In a logic puzzle, each clue rules out some possibilities. A chart keeps track of what you know.

:::example Who plays which sport?
Amara, Ben and Thanh each play a different sport: hockey, curling or soccer. Clue 1: Amara does not play hockey. Clue 2: Ben does not play hockey. Clue 3: Amara does not play curling. Who plays each sport?
@step Clues 1 and 2 cross out hockey for Amara and Ben, so Thanh must play hockey.
@step Clue 3 crosses out curling for Amara. Hockey is taken, so Amara plays soccer.
@step The only sport left for Ben is curling.
@answer Amara plays soccer, Ben plays curling and Thanh plays hockey.
:::

| | hockey | curling | soccer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amara | ✗ (clue 1) | ✗ (clue 3) | ✓ |
| Ben | ✗ (clue 2) | ✓ | ✗ |
| Thanh | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |

:::tip One mark per row and column
Each person has exactly one answer and each answer belongs to exactly one person. When a row or column has only one empty box left, it must be a ✓.
:::

## Games and winning strategies

In a take-away game, players take turns removing counters. Suppose each player may take 1, 2 or 3 counters, and the player who takes the last counter wins. Work backwards: if you leave your opponent exactly 4 counters, whatever they take, you can take the rest. So you want to leave a multiple of 4 every time.

:::example Winning move
There are 15 counters and you go first. How many should you take?
@step You want to leave a multiple of 4. The nearest one below 15 is 12.
@step Take $15 - 12 = 3$ counters.
@step After each turn your opponent takes, take enough to make that round add to 4: you leave 8, then 4, then 0.
@answer Take 3 counters on your first turn.
:::

In the Tower of Hanoi, a stack of discs moves from one peg to another, one disc at a time, never placing a larger disc on a smaller one. With 1, 2, 3 and 4 discs the fewest moves are 1, 3, 7 and 15. The pattern suggests the conjecture $2^n - 1$ moves for $n$ discs. Deductive reasoning shows why each extra disc doubles the moves and adds 1: move the smaller stack off, move the largest disc, then move the smaller stack back on top.

:::summary
- Solve number riddles by listing the possibilities from the strongest clue and crossing them off.
- Use a chart for logic puzzles: each row and each column gets exactly one ✓.
- Work backwards to find a winning strategy in a game.
- Look for patterns (inductive reasoning), then explain them (deductive reasoning), as in the Tower of Hanoi.
- Count systematically, one size or one case at a time.
:::
